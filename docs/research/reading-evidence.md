# Human reading comprehension and processing cost

Compiled on November 20, 2026. This purpose-built bibliography contains **46 empirical papers based on human responses**. It includes 36 papers in the primary 2000–2026 range and 10 foundational experiments from 1980–1999. The project chose 2000 as the start of its primary search window. Separate references address theory, measurement, and reading media. The catalog also identifies resources for dataset construction and reanalysis. Stage 1 records study-by-study observations, source checks, and uses in the model. [Observation registry](stage-1-observations.md)

The search expanded from cross-sentence coherence, prior knowledge, and reading comprehension to psycholinguistics and discourse linguistics. Search terms included `cognitive load`, `mental effort`, `working memory load`, `reading processing load`, `reading comprehension`, `discourse comprehension`, `bridging inference`, `coreference`, and `discourse connectives`. Searches for Mandarin studies also used the Chinese equivalents of causal inference, discourse comprehension, and reference. We added earlier studies based on the research question and citation tracking. We prioritized original studies with identifiable reader groups, text contrasts, and measured endpoints. The bibliography supports study design. Its coverage has not been evaluated systematically.

The catalog identifies **candidate reading effects and experimental contrasts**, with the observation location, participant group, and difference between conditions. The first set prioritizes local concepts, discourse relationships, and inferences. Answering questions, transfer, self-reported mental effort, and eye movements use distinct measurement units. Comprehension responses can calibrate error risk on similar questions. Mental effort uses the corresponding reader ratings. Each row summarizes evidence from one paper. Studies enter training, validation, or replication when the available stimuli, trial data, or comparable statistics support that use.

## Core empirical studies, 2000–2026

### Comprehension, inference, and text revision

| Study | Design and observations | Relevance to CogLLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R04** Gilabert, Martínez & Vidal-Abarca (2005) | Spanish secondary school and university students read texts about the Russian Revolution. In Experiment 1, students given the causal explanation performed better on open-book inference questions than those given the original text. The effect of increasing conceptual overlap alone varied between experiments. | Contrast supplying a missing causal premise with repeating key words. Preserve the order in which participants read and answer. [§2–3, Appendices](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.12.003) · [Detailed records of both experiments](stage-1/coherence-and-knowledge.md) |
| **R05** Van Silfhout, Evers-Vermeul & Sanders (2015) | Dutch eighth graders read narrative and expository texts with or without connectives. With connectives, performance on local bridging-inference questions improved and readers moved through subsequent regions more quickly. | Compare sentence pairs with and without explicit links. Evaluate reading speed and answer accuracy separately. [Results, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2014.905237) |
| **R06** Kendeou & van den Broek (2007) | English-speaking university students first completed an assessment of misconceptions about Newton's laws, then read texts with or without explicit refutations. Prior knowledge and text structure interacted in reading time per syllable of the sentences stating the laws. Recall differed by knowledge, and the effect of text structure on recall was statistically inconclusive. | Test specific misconceptions at the sentences stating the laws. Retain the uncertainty in the recall results. [Experiment 2, Tables 2–3](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03193491.pdf#page=7) |
| **R07** Gu, Leroy & Kauchak (2017) | Adults read English medical excerpts from Wikipedia and PubMed. In Study 1, comprehension accuracy was 62.6% with technical definitions and 72.1% with plain-language explanations, a difference of 9.5 percentage points. Passage reading time showed no statistically significant correlation with comprehension in this study. | Compare explanatory versions using the same comprehension question. Interpret the effect for the passages and readers in this study. [Study 1, Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5977593/) |
| **R08** Cohen et al. (2025) | The authors reanalyzed adult answers from R07. An LLM estimated coherence using sentences, preceding text, and summaries. Some scores correlated with passage-level answer accuracy and improved for some explanatory rewrites. | Use this as a coherence baseline. R07 and R08 share data, and the study specifies the edit locations. [§3.1, §4.2, Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12243615/) |
| **R09** Tibken & Tiffin-Richards (2025) | 96 German readers read 20 short expository texts. A single word changed between versions to create an inconsistency. After reading, participants indicated whether they had detected an inconsistency and selected the sentence containing it. Rereading the target word was associated with correct detection. Evidence from first-pass measures was weaker. | The task explicitly asks readers to detect inconsistencies. The public trial table supports analysis of correctness. Individual sentence selections are unavailable in that table. [Methods, Tables 2–3, Discussion](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-025-09440-2) · [Data and Code](https://osf.io/ekf4t/?view_only=1cd488d47c254163bfc032fbbc7fa0cc) |
| **R10** Beker, Jolles, Lorch & van den Broek (2016) | Dutch university students read two successive passages. The target sentence in the second passage was identical between versions. The first passage either provided or omitted an explanation that could resolve the inconsistency. Readers processed the target sentence faster when the explanation was available. Experiment 2 found no corresponding difference in free recall. | Hold the target text fixed and evaluate subsequent sentences and recall separately. [Experiments 1–2, Table 2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-016-9630-3) |

| **R12** McNamara (2004) | The study compared self-explanation reading training with a read-aloud control. Among readers with low prior knowledge, training improved performance on text-based questions in the mitosis posttest. Bridging-inference questions did not show the same effect. | Define the question types to which the training benefit applies. [Results, Posttraining text, Fig. 1](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326950dp3801_1) |

Higher cohesion improved text-based answers, with larger benefits for more skilled readers in this study. Prior knowledge was positively associated with overall comprehension. | Record question type and the scope of required evidence in training annotations. Evaluate on held-out reader groups. | **R17** Wiley & Rayner (2000) | The study supplied a topic title for an otherwise identical English passage. ### Limits of localization and measurement

| Study | Design and observations | Relevance to CogLLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R22** Wang et al. (2006) | Participants read Chinese text line by line at their own pace. A locally plausible target that contradicted an earlier background passage was read more slowly than an unrelated control. This pattern appeared in versions with and without a syntactic boundary. | Hold local plausibility fixed while varying the distant premise in Chinese cross-sentence contrasts. [Experiment 1, original publication](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlxb/CN/Y2006/V38/I05/645) |
| **R23** Troyer et al. (2024) | The study measured readers' knowledge of particular Harry Potter facts and their overall domain knowledge separately. It recorded ERPs during the keywords of correct answers. Greater domain knowledge was associated with a smaller N400 amplitude even when a reader could not retrieve the specific fact. | Encode concept-level knowledge and domain familiarity separately. Validate any generalization from physiological responses to comprehension answers. [§1.2, Fig. 4, Table 4](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.14422) |
| **R24** Schotter, Tran & Rayner (2014) | English readers read sentences with or without syntactic ambiguity. When a mask prevented effective rereading, comprehension accuracy declined even on unambiguous sentences. | Represent rereading as a possible repair strategy when modeling comprehension failures. [Results, General discussion](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614531148) |
| **R26** Demberg & Sayeed (2016) | In German self-paced reading, a semantic violation slowed reading of the target word. Pupil changes were observed approximately 600–1,100 ms after target presentation. | Use physiology as a supplemental measure and retain its temporal lag. [Experiment 2, Figs. 4–5](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0146194) · [Pupillary data](https://doi.org/10.7910/DVN/QGEAL1) |
| **R27** Timkey et al. (2026) | Temporary syntactic ambiguity in English slowed reading at the disambiguating word and the next word. Surprisal from several LMs was relatively close to the forward-reading effect, while rereading time increased by much more. | Control for word position, spillover, and rereading separately. [Figs. 1, 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13462876/#fig03) · [Data and code](https://osf.io/b6rqh/) |
| **R28** Hirotani, Frazier & Rayner (2006) | The study added a comma marking a clause boundary in an English sentence. In Experiment 1, this slowed local first-pass processing and shortened total sentence reading time. | Test whether punctuation-induced changes in local reading behavior distort the comprehension score. [Experiment 1](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/faces/ViewItemFullPage.jsp?itemId=item_725746_4) |
| **R29** Xue et al. (2026) | A Simplified Chinese eye-tracking study placed the same target word either mid-sentence or at sentence end. The pattern differed across measures, with shorter total fixation time at the sentence-final target. | Test boundary effects separately by language and eye-movement measure. [Fig. 1, Table 4](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470218251347264) · [Data and Code](https://osf.io/xf2q9/) |
| **R30** Smith & Levy (2013) | English eye-tracking and self-paced reading corpora support an association between word surprisal, the negative log of the word's conditional probability, and reading time. The effect can spill over to subsequent words, with different patterns across the two methods. | Use surprisal as a control. Retain current and subsequent word regions in the positional evaluation. [Figs. 3–4](https://www.mit.edu/~rplevy/papers/smith-levy-2013-cognition.pdf) |

### Psycholinguistics and discourse linguistics

| Study | Design and observations | Relevance to CogLLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R31** Christianson, Hollingworth, Halliwell & Ferreira (2001) | English-speaking adults read sentences with temporary syntactic ambiguity. In Experiment 2, readers made more errors on questions about subordinate-clause roles and answered most main-clause role questions correctly. Erroneous interpretations could therefore persist after disambiguation. | Test each semantic relation of the same sentence separately. Record the ambiguity source, disambiguation region, and the role readers misinterpreted. [Experiment 2, Table 5](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf) |
| **R32** Camblin, Gordon & Swaab (2007) | The study manipulated global discourse coherence and local lexical association for target words in English passages. Distant discourse coherence affected eye movements and ERPs even when the sentence was locally interpretable. | Hold local semantic plausibility fixed and vary earlier context to test discourse integration. Validate answer accuracy separately. [Experiments 1–5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1766924/) |
| **R33** Claus & Kelter (2006) | Flashbacks changed the order in which events appeared in the text. Events that occurred earlier in story time were harder to retrieve even when a flashback had recently mentioned them. Experiment 3 used a recognition test. | Distinguish textual distance from distance in story time. Evaluate the recognition endpoint used in the original study. [Experiments 1–4](https://research.uni-hannover.de/en/publications/comprehending-narratives-containing-flashbacks-evidence-for-tempo/) |
| **R34** Cozijn, Noordman & Vonk (2011) | Dutch readers read sentence pairs with or without causal connectives. Explicit causal marking sped up processing immediately after the connective and slowed processing at the end of the clause. In Experiment 2, it also sped verification of an inference statement. | Compare measurement locations and response times for integration during reading and inference after reading. [Experiments 1–2, original](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/files/1442185/cozijn_noordman_vonk2011.pdf) |
| **R35** Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders (2019) | 794 adolescents read 20 authentic Dutch texts with manipulated connectives. In local cloze questions, explicit causal and contrastive links improved performance, and explicit additive links reduced performance. The study found no effect when aggregating across the full texts. | Allow weak coherence and redundant cues to have different effects. Evaluate local cloze performance and full-text performance separately. [Results, author institution full text](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/comprehension-effects-of-connectives-across-texts-readers-and-coh/) |
| **R36** Zhan, Levy & Kehler (2020) | Mandarin discourse-continuation experiments manipulated active, ba, and bei constructions as well as implicit causality. Syntactic form and discourse expectations shifted the distribution of potential pronoun referents. | The three released CSV files contain 1,651, 2,334, and 2,741 participant continuations, respectively, with original text, manipulation condition, and the referent mentioned again. Each released referent code identifies one of two candidates. Analyze the referent distribution in the retained responses. [Experiments 1–3](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0237012) · [Experiment 1 CSV](https://osf.io/download/8zk57/) · [Experiment 2 CSV](https://osf.io/download/z48qy/) · [Experiment 3 CSV](https://osf.io/download/yajhg/) |
| **R37** Wu & Mo (2010) | Three self-paced reading experiments used Chinese expository texts. When readers could retrieve the premises, both explicit and implicit premises elicited an inference signal at the conclusion sentence. Inserting two sentences weakened the effect. | Use the original premise-distance manipulation and conclusion location to test the self-paced reading effect. Accuracy on post-reading problem solving lacks a corresponding observation. [Experiments 1–3, original publication](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlxb/CN/abstract/abstract210.shtml) |
| **R38** Scholman, Yan, Yung & Demberg (2026) | Two Mandarin self-paced reading experiments manipulated paired causal and concessive connectives. The first marker produced no replicable facilitation at the second marker in reading times. | Use this as a negative control for a model that assigns decreases in difficulty whenever a connective appears. Reading time was the primary endpoint. [§4–6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-026-10269-2) · [Data and Code](https://osf.io/frh6z) |
| **R39** Yu, Hsiao & Lin (2025) | Native Mandarin speakers read short dialogues containing classifiers, connectives, or gradable adjectives and chose a continuation. Preliminary results suggest that the effect of context on literal and pragmatic interpretations varied by word class. | Hold phrases such as “some” fixed. Test how context changes the distribution of “some but not all” inferences. [§3–4, original publication](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/cld.25002.yu) |
| **R40** Wei et al. (2024) | The interaction of syntactic and semantic information in Mandarin ba sentences differed across natural eye-tracking and self-paced reading. | Compare local Mandarin effects across measurement methods. [Experiments 1–2, Tables 3–4](https://doi.org/10.1017/langcog.2023.42) · [Data](https://osf.io/5vfwr/) |
| **R41** Bian, Yoon & Komachi (2026) | Adults read English medical abstracts or plain-language rewrites. The study collected sentence-level self-ratings of comprehension and processing ease, and answers to multiple-choice questions with access to the full text. | Use sentence-level supervision or test whether self-ratings track the risk of incorrect answers. Answer evidence identifies supporting sentences. Locating difficulty while reading requires a separate measure. [Data and Methods](https://aclanthology.org/2026.readi-1.4/) |

### Unresolved information, later cues, and comprehension updates

| Study | Design and observations | Relevance to CogLLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R44** Love, McKoon & Gerrig (2010) | When the narrative introduced a character without clarifying their connection to the story, readers recognized the character's name faster in a probe task. Recognition responses to subsequent words slowed, and memory for subsequent information weakened. | Measure the accessibility of unresolved information and its effect on subsequent processing separately. [Experiments 1–4, Tables 4–5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864939/) |
| **R45** Jones & Bergen (2024) | English passages varied whether a pronoun was ambiguous and whether later text fit the more common interpretation. Experiment 1 collected referent choices. In Experiment 2, violating the more common interpretation slowed reading in a later region. An unambiguous version controlled for differences in the continuation. | Compare earlier interpretations with where difficulty appears later. The experiments pair materials across studies. | **R46** Kendeou, Smith & O’Brien (2013) | When a causal sentence followed the refutation, the authors still observed reactivation of old information and found no measurable downstream comprehension interference. | **R47** Pettijohn & Radvansky (2016) | The narratives varied whether an upcoming event transition was signaled. In Experiment 3, an unannounced transition slowed local reading. ## Foundational empirical studies, 1980–1999

| Study | Design and observations | Relevance to CogLLM |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **R42** Daneman & Carpenter (1980) | The authors developed the reading-span measure and examined its relation to recall of facts and pronoun reference. | Use as an early measure of working memory and individual differences in comprehension. Obtain individual scores through measurement. [Original study ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ230538) |
| **R43** Yeung, Jin & Sweller (1998) | Five reading experiments compared formats such as explanations integrated with the text and glossary lists. The effect of integrated explanations on comprehension went in opposite directions for native-speaking fifth graders and adults. English learners with lower and higher proficiency also showed different patterns. | Hold the terms fixed while varying how explanations are presented. Assess text comprehension and vocabulary learning separately. [Experiments 1–5](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9514686/) |
| **R01** McNamara, E. Kintsch, Songer & W. Kintsch (1996) | English-speaking adolescents read expository texts about heart disease with varying levels of local and global coherence. In Experiment 2, prior knowledge and coherence interacted on inference and problem-solving questions. The study analyzed inference, problem solving, classification, and text recall separately. | Evaluate by question type and prior knowledge level. Preserve the length differences between original and rewritten texts. [Experiment 2, Fig. 4, Appendix B](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1) |
| **R02** McNamara & Kintsch (1996) | Readers with higher or lower prior knowledge read expository history texts that varied in coherence. Readers with higher prior knowledge performed better on keyword classification with the low-coherence version. Comprehension results varied by question type. | Retain conditions in which readers may benefit from making inferences themselves. [Two experiments, Results](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539609544975) |
| **R03** Britton & Gülgöz (1991) | The authors used a model of comprehension to locate places in an instructional text where readers had to supply inferences, then rewrote those places together. College students recalled more from the revised text. In a separate experiment, their representation of concept relations was closer to that of experts. | Use as a precedent for repairing inferential gaps. The study estimates the combined effect of the edits. Individual locations need separate measurement. [Experiments 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329) |
| **R11** Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994) | Eighth graders read an expository text about the human circulatory system. The group prompted to self-explain each line gained more knowledge between pretest and posttest. The study also examined complex questions, implicit component functions, and mental models. | Use self-explanations to elicit readers' inferences. Distinguish performance under prompting from unaided reading. [Methods, Results](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3) |
| **R13** Recht & Leslie (1988) | All 64 middle school students read the same account of a half-inning of baseball. Groups were formed from prior tests of baseball knowledge and reading ability. Knowledge was associated with reenactment, oral recall, summary, and categorization by importance. | Compare domain knowledge groups on the same text. The effect of briefly teaching that knowledge requires a separate experiment. [Methods, Results](https://www.yesataretelearningtrust.net/Portals/0/Effect-of-Prior-Knowledge-on-Good-and-Poor-Readers-Memory-of-Text.pdf) |
| **R15** McKeown, Beck, Sinatra & Loxterman (1992) | Schoolchildren read original or more coherent versions of historical texts. Improved coherence increased recall and comprehension scores. The study also compared readers with different prior knowledge. | Design rewrites that clarify the causal sequence of events. Record textual changes and prior knowledge separately. [Full author manuscript, Results](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED410308.pdf) · [Journal version](https://doi.org/10.2307/747784) |
| **R19** Harp & Mayer (1998) | The experiments added interesting details unrelated to the learning objective to instructional materials about lightning formation. Such details could reduce main-idea recall and transfer. In Experiment 4, the effect depended on where the details appeared. | Compare explanatory additions with equally long tangential additions. The original materials had words and pictures. Transfer to text-only materials requires separate measurement. [Experiments 1–4](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.90.3.414) |
| **R21** Albrecht & O’Brien (1993) | A critical sentence in a narrative was either consistent or inconsistent with an earlier character description. In Experiment 1, readers slowed at the critical sentence when it was inconsistent. | Record the observation location and the earlier sentence that gives rise to the inconsistency separately. [Experiment 1](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.19.5.1061) |

## Theory, measurement, and reading media

- **Sweller (1988)** uses problem solving and schema acquisition to explain how limited processing resources affect learning. This paper provides context for cognitive load theory. The bibliography draws on direct reading experiments to design text manipulations. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1202_4)
- **Paas, Tuovinen, Tabbers & van Gerven (2003)** review ratings of mental effort, behavioral measures, and physiological measures. The review informs measurement selection and endpoint interpretation. [Original paper](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15326985EP3801_8)
- **DeLeeuw & Mayer (2008)** studied instruction with an animated electric motor. Secondary-task response time, self-reported mental effort, and difficulty ratings differed in their sensitivity to the interventions. This informs the choice of measures. Their validity for text reading requires separate evaluation. [Experiments 1–2, Table 6](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.100.1.223)
- **Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmerón (2018)** conducted a meta-analysis of print and digital reading comparisons from 2000–2017. They reported differences according to time constraints and genre. This informs how we define medium and timing conditions. The outcome was comprehension. [Author manuscript, abstract and methods](https://www.uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado%202018%20dont%20throw%20away%20your%20printed%20books.pdf)

Available data support sentence-level self-ratings, sentence-level problem categories, marked locations where material use permits, and post-reading comprehension. Sentence-end reports, full-text selections, and coarser follow-up intervals can support validation at their original resolution. ## How to use effects reported in papers

- **When original observations are available**, retain the article, variant, participant or group, reading instructions, question type, target region, endpoint, sample size, and data source. The original study and reanalysis share a split group. R07 and R08 use the same data.
- **When only published statistics are available**, record the direction, estimate, unit, and reported uncertainty. These summaries define replication targets or plausible effect ranges. A condition difference in inference-question accuracy comes from participant answers. It supplies no word-level intensity labels.
- **When only materials and experimental controls are available**, reconstruct stimuli, interventions, and model inputs. Without corresponding existing human responses, the experiment can verify engineering behavior or generate hypotheses. Report any change in reader population, language, or question type.
- **When results are statistically inconclusive**, retain the uncertainty or flag them for replication. A claim that two conditions are similar requires a predefined equivalence interval with practical meaning and a test with adequate precision.
