# human oral and read-back data verification

When only the body text is available, question localization requires the reader's report and the currently displayed original text. Tracking the same question also requires linking subsequent reports back to prior questions, and distinguishing between "unanswered" and "unobserved" reading stop records. The following source verification covers the actual granularity of the original document and its usage conditions.

## Another English keyboard dataset

Paape and Vasishth (2026) `fixations.Rda` were downloaded from the author archive and verified for fields, comprising 319 readers, 36 stimulus sets, and six conditions, totaling 197,822 observed events. Grouping by `subj` and `item` yields 11,480 trials, each with a unique `fixno` belonging to a single condition and occurring from the onset of the segment. The key field `dir` and segment `no` allow reconstruction of stepwise forward, backward, and restart operations. Additionally, four restarts within segment one remained in segment one during construction, requiring correspondence to their original actions. The authors also publicly released the `alt_stim.txt` for the 36 stimulus sets. The SHA-256 hash of `fixations.Rda` is `e7bde8f75569f98942671e3d1344ca5bd0b7348c23cda206d2dc7aacd6cfdbab`, and `alt_stim.txt` is `7e7cf3581de3f7ec988253ba6adf175390c7ca5b6583ab79597880ed19de3f22`. The archive is licensed under CC BY-NC 4.0. This verification currently covers only the record structure, without text overlap, full segment alignment, or condition-wise scoring. [Paper](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749596X26000185) · [Data and Stimuli](https://osf.io/6uv37/) · [Archive License Field](https://api.osf.io/v2/licenses/60bf992258510b0009a5a9a6/)

This data is suitable for independent English readout scoring on first keystroke, and possesses a design capable of distinguishing antecedent conditions. Whether the same query is discovered or resolved internally still requires oral reporting or sequential reporting. [Current English-German readout observation specifications and validation](first-readback-transfer-results.md)

## Reader-initiated stop data

Li et al. (2026) had English L1 and Chinese L1 English L2 readers read the same set of English materials paragraph by paragraph. Each sentence had five display regions. readers pressed `J` to continue or `F` to end that reading session. The OSF `L1_region2.csv` and `L2_region2.csv` retained segmented sentences, `region_all_keys`, `region_last_key`, and trial ID columns. All 160 versions of the stimulus table could reconstruct five paragraphs. For each condition, cross-checking the first 16 KiB of complete data rows yielded 86 and 85 matches, with 9 and 15 instances of `F`. Among these, `j,j,f` confirmed that the trial stopped at the third region. A strict filter check of 88 corresponding rows found all to be five `J`. The identified files contained reaction time analysis data, lacking raw logs covering all scheduled trials. Data could identify retained trial stopping regions but were missing complete risk denominators for the first two regions and did not track comprehension post-stopping. Neither the OSF project nor subprojects included data authorization. [Original Study Method](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716426100939) · [Author Archive](https://osf.io/fx3mr/) · [L1 File](https://osf.io/k6zba/) · [L2 File](https://osf.io/vkmyr/) · [Stimulus Table](https://osf.io/download/vayus/)

Singh et al. (2016) instructed readers to actively press a key to indicate whether to stop across nine sequentially presented segments. Two experiments manipulated contextual support for definite noun phrases and `too`. Appendix stimuli are publicly available. The paper can be used to fix the condition effect and critical segment. We consulted publisher, author, and laboratory data repositories but have not yet obtained the raw table used to reconstruct the per-trial stop rate. [Procedures and Public Appendix](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/cogs.12260)

Other sequential selection data from the same reader, compared against pre-training, are stored in [human reference updates during gradual text reveal](written-reference-update-source.md).

## Queries and responses to social annotations

Zhou compiled 2,000 course annotations from Lacuna Stories. The data includes readers, selected spans, articles, and strategy information, with perplexity derived from research coders. A smaller-scale experiment collected reader self-assessments and selection coordinates. Existing papers and open projects do not provide the original trials for these two batches, nor does the documentation record the read position at the time of questioning and subsequent confirmation. [Paper §4.1–4.2](https://tongyuzhou.com/files/tongyu_zhou_undergrad_thesis.pdf#page=29)

Miller et al.'s Perusall includes text highlighting, discussion threads, and "helpful" tags, allowing readers to reply directly from notification emails. Highlighting can pinpoint the target text of published questions, while tags can be assigned by other readers. timestamps indicate only the chronological order of interactions. The authors' and school's public archives do not provide complete links to highlighted segments, subsequent confirmation by questioners, and the original text as it was visible at the time. [Platform methods and Figs. 3, 5, 6](https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2018.00008) · [Author archives](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/73120379-2ee6-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b)

The four text learning experiments conducted by List and Lin, along with subsequent analyses utilizing the same dataset, provide a frequency count of the 774 selected annotations and illustrative examples. The public release does not include the ability to reconstruct individual readers' selection, query initiation, response, and confirmation trajectories. [Original Experiment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2023.104791) · [Follow-up Analysis and Table 1](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2024/proceedings/2024.EDM-posters.74/index.html)

## Explicit contradiction report and co-respondent re-answer

Tibken and Tiffin-Richards (2025) manipulated a target word across 20 German explanatory passages. Readers reported whether the passage contained a contradiction. after reporting a contradiction, they selected a sentence from the full text re-displayed. The public analysis table includes data from 94 readers across 1,870 trials (935 per condition), retaining the target word, position, and a composite correct-response column. For the contradiction condition, a correct response required both detecting and selecting the target sentence. The original report and the specific incorrect selection cannot be reconstructed from the composite column. Only one set of German stimuli with English examples is available in the appendix. The paper is licensed under CC BY 4.0, and the condition for standalone use of the public analysis file is not specified. [Study materials and reporting rules](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11409-025-09440-2) · [Author information and examples](https://osf.io/ekf4t/?view_only=1cd488d47c254163bfc032fbbc7fa0cc)

Dersch et al. (2026) analyzed the ability of the syntax to identify a reader's item-by-item pre-post responses, confidence, and conditions. 336 readers were assigned to four feedback conditions. the article appendix provides two narrative and English feedback versions. Pre-post testing covered full text exposure, constituting a discourse-level update. The visible English appendix must be cross-referenced with the actual German stimuli used in testing, and the conditions for reusing raw analysis data remain to be confirmed. [Research and Syntax Analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-026-10116-9) · [Stimulus Appendix](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-026-10116-9/tables/4) · [Author Archive](https://osf.io/tp2sn/)

Staub et al. (2025) had readers read news one page at a time and click where they detected a missing, repeated, or transposed function word. The public table contains 165 readers included in the analysis, with 9 targets each, yielding 1,485 scorable trials and 342 hits. Of the 215 participants who finished, 37 were excluded because they never clicked throughout the task. The separate file with 2,505 click positions records screen coordinates without reader IDs or timestamps. The 72 targets can be aligned to images of the original display, with character offsets unavailable and reuse terms for third-party newspaper text still to be verified. This source directly measures clicks at error locations with the whole page visible. The records cannot identify the first prefix that triggered a report. [Original methods](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-024-02586-1) · [Reader-level table](https://osf.io/download/k4caq/) · [Target coordinates](https://osf.io/download/47pt2/) · [Screen clicks](https://osf.io/download/5azxh/)

Rodger et al.'s policy evidence experiment has been validated to precisely connect the same reader, the same proposition, and two numerical reports. It provides a page-level update endpoint. [Data and Scope](rodger-belief-source.md)

Frick and Schüler's text-only condition preserved full sentence order and keystrokes per sentence, allowing reconstruction of the first explicitly signaled sentence-level reading stop. Each version of the identical target sentence had 900 trials. [File verification and no-issue endpoint](frick-consistency-source.md)

Singer et al. conducted English experiments that queried sentence-by-sentence consistency. the public data include the correctness of target sentences and the response time of subsequent sentences. Published results can be compared to the magnitude of the target sentence report rate. Sentence-by-key responses and a full stimulus-word count comparison were not obtained. [File Verification and Sample Differences](singer-validation-source.md)

The authors of Currie et al. can reconstruct the English children's end-of-passage explicit contradiction report from the stimulus and individual responses. There were 24 near-far contradiction topics with 4,248 mutually alignable end-of-passage reports, of which 20 pairs differed only in the order of complete sentences. The end-of-passage question did not measure the first noticed reading stop. [Stimulus and report alignment](larrc-consistency-source.md)

Florit et al.'s Italian conflict detection used 12 short stories, with children receiving a written version while listening. The authors summed the performance on detecting two conflicting sentences, and the data were reported sequentially according to data availability statements for application purposes. Braasch et al. investigated cross-text conflicts. an archived SPSS table from the authors contains 102 participant records, retaining aggregated conflict memory and reading time per participant, while lacking the original text, item-by-item sentence selection, and their positions. [Florit et al. §2.2.3 and data statement](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeo.2025.100243) · [Braasch et al. public archive](https://doi.org/10.17632/rsfc5gdbh3.1)

Hoffman (1980) had 41 readers read five short passages in segments and repeatedly guess the same noun replaced by a pseudoword at each reading stop. This design allows observation of when a reader updates their word meaning judgment. The article includes a set of stimulus examples and group summaries, but no publicly available data have been found that allow recalculation of sequential trajectories. [Original Study 1, Figure 1, and Table 1](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/10862968009547387)

The online reader experiment in GRACE asked readers to re-answer the same question after each cue and report whether they were prepared to answer. Among the authors' publicly archived files, 12 were live competition buzzer records. The online readers' sequential reading stop reports were not found in the public archive. [Original §3.2.1 and Appendix F](https://aclanthology.org/2025.acl-long.962/) · [Author Archive](https://github.com/Pinafore/advcalibration)

Wang et al.'s sentence-by-sentence guessing experiment released 195 readers and 4,680 trials. Each trial records the first step at which the participant answered correctly, or the step at which they failed to answer by step six. In the authors' game, sentences are randomly sampled from the candidate corpus at each step. the public trial table omits the sentence ID displayed in that turn, making it impossible to map the first correct response to character coordinates of individual sentences. [Original and Public Table Columns](core-vocabulary-source.md)

The German ACT by Löffler and Topolinski assigns a fixed word order within the vocabulary to each reader. The public materials and trial table allow the retrieval of the first correct response step for 1,321 recorded trials, along with 678 fifteen-step right-limited trials and one missing correct response step. A skipable input box is placed after each word, thereby targeting the explicit response in the fixed reading task. [Materials and Response Alignment](act-source.md) · [Fixed Readout External Held-out Results](act-transfer-results.md)

Unger et al. manipulated word meaning cues before or after pseudowords. The public dataset includes 77 readers, 1,232 trials with precise matching to author stimuli for image selection accuracy. Correct responses for cues before and after were 448/616 and 449/616, respectively. Image selection was measured after grouped reading, with online word data from eye-tracking. no same-reader word meaning selection across different word prefixes. Zaka et al. published a pure reading group of 45 participants, 1,800 two-sentence trials, and post-response confidence. The study arranged the second sentence of the same pseudoword into compatible or incompatible contexts, with sentences separated by other trials. Released results tables record sentence pairs, versions, accuracy, and confidence, excluding guesses made after reading the first sentence. [Unger et al. procedure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12187554/#S13) · [Unger's trial-by-trial table and stimuli](https://osf.io/2prvy/) · [Zaka et al. procedure](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10224217/3/Krishnan_2026_04_01_AudVis-RWL_v7.pdf) · [Zaka's trial-by-trial table](https://osf.io/download/yc65j/)

## Public instances of the same reader spontaneously asking questions and confirming understanding

The English narrative excerpts from Jiménez, García, and Pearson are listed in Appendix A. The results page records a reader spontaneously asking for the meaning of an adverb in the third sentence of the main text, subsequently quoting the fourth sentence to explain the reasons for the character's mention of cruelty and foolishness, and indicating comprehension. The oral sequence of the target word and the following sentence is retrievable. Self-reported understanding lacks an independent word meaning test. Appendix F presents a selected initial segment strategy, without the denominator of all readers, discourse, and reading stop query risks. [Original report page 13 and Appendices A, F](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED376433.pdf#page=17)

Alsheikh's English academic report includes an instance where a reader spontaneously asked the physician why they held a reserved attitude toward the intervention described in the text. The author recorded that after reading the following two paragraphs, the reader stated a reason, expressed understanding, and then immediately asked another question. Appendix C retains the English text. Subsequent paragraph intervals can be located, but the first token with confidence was not recorded. Another reader reported understanding a proprietary term after continuing to read, with the excerpt not specifying where the stop occurred. These cases can be distinguished as "the same question being resolved" versus "a new question being posed after resolution," and oral sampling cannot estimate the full occurrence rate. The third-party stimulus text reuse condition from both studies has not yet been verified. [Thesis pages 67–69 and Appendix C](https://www.academia.edu/87545821/Metacognitive_Reading_Strategies_Used_by_Native_Speakers_of_Arabic_When_Reading_Academic_Texts_in_Arabic_and_English) · [Author journal methods](https://doi.org/10.5539/elt.v4n2p151)

## Post-hoc inference research

Mulligan and Rawlins collected 100 annotated pairs from ten verbatim interview transcripts. At each step, both the preceding sentence and the target sentence were displayed. readers wrote down the question the target sentence answered and selected the corresponding answer, or reported that no clear question existed. This procedure assessed the association between the answer phrase and the question readers generated afterward. Whether the question existed prior to seeing the answer, and whether the same reader solved it at that sentence, were not measured by this procedure. Two author papers with interfaces and examples were found, but no complete individual public annotations were available. [Formal Study §3](https://journals.linguisticsociety.org/proceedings/index.php/ELM/article/view/5828) · [Primary Data Collection Study](https://openpublishing.library.umass.edu/scil/article/2228/galley/2153/download/)

Cui et al.'s GUIDINGQ incorporates the guiding questions authored in the original text, and compares the presence or absence of these questions across 45 readers and five textbooks. The "press-next" function advances the text segment by segment. Reading comprehension is assessed via post-text summaries, while question quality and utility are evaluated through itemized surveys. The authors have made the guiding question corpus and experimental interface publicly available. This design draws on the discourse function of guiding questions but does not capture the progressive state of readers' self-generated questions. [Original paper §6, Appendix C](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.632/) · [Author-released content](https://github.com/eth-lre/engage-your-readers#human-study)
