# The first correct response position of the German anchor span

In Löffler and Topolinski's primary ACT experiment, 100 native German speakers worked through the same 20 word lists in randomized order. Each list presented 15 uppercase cues in a fixed order, one at a time, for two seconds each. On each step, a reader could guess or skip. The trial ended at the first correct guess, or at step 15 if there was none. This design maps the first correct response step to the specific word presented. We test whether the prefix-based readout trained on the English Core Vocabulary data can distinguish when readers first guess correctly on new German material. [Original study, materials and procedure, and results](https://reference-global.com/article/10.5334/joc.254?tab=article) · [Authors' data and materials](https://osf.io/et5kb/)

## Input and Endpoint

Following the experimental retention of all 20 wordlists, preserving the order and case of the 15 items per list. Each input is joined by newlines, providing only the cue words while removing the answer, wordlist title, step-by-step guesses, and subject columns. For each held-out list, perform a causal forward pass on the candidate checkpoint, reading out the final complete tokenizer token for each displayed word. The remote ID is used solely for retrieving results. First, verify the prefix tokenization of all character-level reading stops. additionally perform suffix replacement, prefix-only runs, padding, and suffix difference positive control. The word positions in ACT are observable from the original experiment, while sub-token positions within words do not correspond to human responses.

Answers are judged by the author's confirmed success state and the first ordinal correct response. Trials that succeed but lack an ordinal are marked as unknown and excluded from both denominators. Trials first correct after step six are right-censored in the six-step analysis, while trials never correct are also right-censored. The five-step analysis right-censors only trials that remain incorrect at the final step. The model's hazard represents the estimated probability of first correct response upon reading the current word, conditional on not yet being incorrect. Each step's hazard is multiplied by the prior probability of remaining incorrect to form the event mass, which is then combined with the final-censored mass for scoring.

## Cross-study validation of fixed models

The primary analysis utilized only the first six steps, fully freezing the English Core Vocabulary across three readout groups: normalized features, intercepts, and slopes. The control group retained the original step count and literal features, while the experimental group added a single 128-dimensional Qwen projection. Each ACT vocabulary list contained only one original path, with stepwise probabilities scored directly along that path. Model predictions were first saved and then combined with ACT human responses. The two primary comparisons were the first correct responses and constrained NLL across 20 vocabulary lists with equal weighting, measured in nats per trial (lower is better). Improvements by Qwen over both controls, along with the vocabulary pairing 95% confidence intervals, must be entirely above zero. Additionally, cumulative correct response rates at steps 1, 3, and 6 were used for magnitude validation, requiring the model's pairing confidence intervals relative to human performance to fall entirely within ±5 percentage points. Vocabulary lists were resampled with equal weighting 10,000 times using seed 735972, and vocabulary–reader cross-validation confidence intervals were also reported.

## Compute the temporal lag between the word lists in the fifteen-step check

Supplementary analysis re-estimated the per-step intercepts for each of the three readout groups using alternative ACT vocabularies, while retaining the English-fitted slopes and feature scales for both text readout groups. Following the five-fold split of the sealed snapshot, each fold estimated 15 intercepts, with the final fold reserved for scoring. Each step minimized the risk set binary negative log-likelihood under equal weighting across vocabularies, incorporating a virtual trial with zero text offset, assigned half an event and half a non-event. Each group independently fitted the same number of intercepts. This comparison assesses whether adding Qwen can still distinguish the response distribution across vocabularies after the baseline accuracy across tasks changes. Scoring utilized full-curve NLL, with additional reporting of cumulative deviations at steps 3, 6, 10, and 15. A supplementary interval was estimated via resampling with fixed out-of-fold predictions, quantifying the variance of these predictions across observed vocabularies and readers.

ACT provides a verifiable event defined as "the position of the first correct response by human readers after reading the $n$-th known cue in a cloze task." In ordinary texts, readers may spontaneously form questions within a paragraph or possess understanding without reporting it. these two conditions can yield different human response distribution endpoints. The data supports the former endpoint, while the original study did not measure the latter. The semantic ordering of cues was fixed by the authors. both analyses were used for prediction, and a single vocabulary prevents estimating the causal effect of rewriting or reordering. The data predates model release, allowing evaluation of verifiable study splits and supervised isolation, though whether the model's pre-training exposed it to the source remains unknown.
