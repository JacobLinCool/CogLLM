# Stage 1 · Text Coherence and Reader Knowledge

These 12 studies provide three observations for comparison. Text revisions can change inference-question scores. Reader knowledge and reading tasks can change the direction of a difference. Local reading times and post-reading performance can show different results within one study. The table retains the stimulus locations, when responses were collected, and scoring units. Full reusable trial-level data have yet to be verified for these sources, so their current uses are study design and aggregate comparison. Where appendices are available, the table records how much of the materials can be reconstructed.

| **R01** McNamara et al. (1996) | Experiment 2 included 56 students aged 10–15 about to enter grades 7–10, with 14 readers per version of an English expository text about heart disease. The researchers measured topic knowledge before reading, used a median split, and crossed manipulations of local references, inferential links, and global structure. Both reading rounds ended with recall and questions. | The interaction of knowledge with the highest- versus lowest-coherence texts on problem-solving questions was `F(1,24) = 5.5`. In the lowest- and highest-coherence versions, the proportions of points earned were .61 and .46 for high-knowledge readers and .17 and .30 for low-knowledge readers, respectively. The interaction went in the same direction for bridging questions, `F(1,24) = 4.8`. The coherence difference on text-based questions did not reach statistical significance, `p = .11`. [Experiment 2, Table 2, Fig. 4, pp.20–29](https://www.academia.edu/783348/Are_Good_Texts_Always_Better_Interactions_of_Text_Coherence_Background_Knowledge_and_Levels_of_Understanding_In_Learning_From_Text) | **Design and aggregate comparison.** Appendix B prints the two extreme versions and marks where the two types of additions occurred. Methods and tables are available. The full question set, trial-level data, and permission for machine-learning reuse have yet to be located. Comparisons need to account for question type and knowledge level. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci1401_1) |
| **R02** McNamara & Kintsch (1996) | High- and low-knowledge readers read English history texts that differed in coherence. Experiment 1 used post-reading free recall, multiple-choice questions, and keyword sorting. Experiment 2 used open-ended questions and sorting, measured immediately and after one week. The journal abstract was checked. Sample sizes and exact stimulus locations remain unverified. | In Experiment 1, the more coherent version produced better recall and multiple-choice performance, while high-knowledge readers scored better on sorting after the less coherent version. Experiment 2 did not reproduce the sorting result. High-knowledge readers scored better on open-ended questions after the less coherent version. The abstract provides no estimates or intervals. [Journal abstract, Experiments 1–2](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01638539609544975) | **Design.** Treat recall, sorting, and open-ended responses separately. Full materials, trial-level data, and reuse permission remain unverified. Keep R02 and R03 in a related stimulus family until their versions can be checked. | **R03** Britton & Gülgöz (1991) | The researchers used a comprehension model to identify passages requiring inference in an English expository text of about a thousand words, then revised the text as a whole. Experiment 1 included 170 college students. U.S. Air Force recruits also read the original or revised version, with the conceptual structures of the author and seven domain experts used as references. | The abstract reports better post-reading free recall after the revised version. Correlations between recruits' and the author or experts' conceptual structures exceeded .50 for the revised text and were about .10 for the original. The published abstract is publicly accessible. Exact denominators, confidence intervals, and location-specific effects remain unverified. [Journal abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.83.3.329) · [Archived abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374666093_Using_Kintsch%27s_Computational_Model_to_Improve_Instructional_Text_Effects_of_Repairing_Inference_Calls_on_Recall_and_Cognitive_Structures) | **Design.** The original study evaluated the whole revision. Local contrasts would need separate comprehension questions for each region. Full stimuli, trial-level data, and reuse permission remain unverified. This source shares a connection to the Vietnam War materials with R02. [R04, p.47](https://www.academia.edu/57360367/Some_good_texts_are_always_better_text_revision_to_foster_inferences_of_readers_with_high_and_low_prior_background_knowledge) |
| **R04** Gilabert et al. (2005) | In Experiment 1, 54 grade 8 students in Valencia, Spain read an original Russian Revolution text, a version with conceptual overlap, or a version with causal explanations. A prior lesson manipulated relevant background knowledge. Students read for 18 minutes and recalled freely immediately and one week later. In the later session, they consulted the text to answer the same seven open-ended inference questions. Experiment 2 used college students grouped by existing knowledge. | In Experiment 1, inference totals were 13.2 after causal explanations and 9.2 after the original, out of 21, with within-group SDs of 3.3 and 3.6. The comparison was `F(1,32) = 13.248, p < .01`. The conceptual-overlap version did not differ significantly from the original in that experiment. In Experiment 2, the causal-explanation and original versions scored 15.7 and 11.8, `F(1,57) = 54.185, p < .01`. The conceptual-overlap version also improved inference-question performance in Experiment 2. [Experiment 1 §§2.1–2.2, Experiment 2 §3.2, pp.51–64](https://www.academia.edu/57360367/Some_good_texts_are_always_better_text_revision_to_foster_inferences_of_readers_with_high_and_low_prior_background_knowledge) | **Evaluation candidate, currently used for aggregate design.** The appendix gives the original text and the locations of both types of additions in English. The version used in the study, the full question set, and trial-level data remain unverified, as does permission to reuse the materials. Both experiments and the cited Russian Revolution passages from Vidal-Abarca et al. (2000) belong to the same stimulus family. [Journal article and appendix, pp.66–67](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2004.12.003) |
| **R05** van Silfhout et al. (2015) | The study included 141 Dutch grade 8 students aged 12–15. Each read two narrative texts and two expository texts, seeing one version of each with or without connectives. Online measures covered the connective region, subsequent regions, and regressions. Readers answered local bridging and ordering questions after the texts and a brief distractor activity. | The proportion of points earned on local bridging questions was **.05** lower for implicit than explicit relations, with model `SE = .01` and `F(1,398) = 16.15, p < .001`. The model did not detect a coherence effect on ordering or a coherence × reading ability interaction for bridging questions. The endpoint was the proportion of points earned per reader and text. [Methods and Results, Table 3, pp.54–61](https://research-portal.uu.nl/files/9022988/0163853x.2014.905237.pdf#page=15) | **Evaluation candidate, currently used for aggregate design.** Appendices A and B include representative narrative and expository passages, questions, and ordering tasks. The full Dutch texts, individual responses, and reuse permission remain unconfirmed. A study can manipulate the between-sentence relation marked by a connective and retain the original bridging question as its outcome. [Journal article and appendices](https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2014.905237) |
| **R06** Kendeou & van den Broek (2007) | Experiment 2 included 60 native English-speaking college students. Of 69 recruits, 9 were excluded because English was not their native language. The researchers used a 60% cutoff on the Force Concept Inventory and crossed refutation and nonrefutation texts for Newton's 1st and 3rd laws. Participants read one sentence at a time at their own pace, then completed free recall. The analyses distinguished sentences stating the laws from explanatory sentences. | Reading times for sentences stating the laws were measured in milliseconds per syllable. Means for the misconception and no-misconception groups were 252 and 148 in the refutation condition, and 280 and 310 in the nonrefutation condition. The interaction was `F(1,44) = 8.3, p = .006, η² = .16`. The misconception group recalled less, `t(54) = 2.5, p < .05`. The other main effects and interactions for recall were all `p > .2`. [Experiment 2, Tables 2–3, pp.1572–1573](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BF03193491.pdf#page=7) | **Design.** The appendix gives example versions. Complete trial-level reading times for law statements, recall coding, paired materials, and reuse permission remain unverified. Experiment 1 used think-aloud reading and Experiment 2 used silent reading with the same materials, but the endpoints and samples differed. Sentence reading time serves as an auxiliary measure of processing cost. [Methods and appendix](https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03193491) |
| **R10** Beker et al. (2016) | Experiment 1 included 27 Dutch-fluent college students and 30 fictional topics. Participants read two consecutive short texts at their own pace, one sentence at a time. In the inconsistent conditions, the second text's penultimate sentence was the fixed target, and the first text either provided an explanation or omitted it. In Experiment 2, 32 participants recalled the texts after reading four text pairs. The experiment also crossed the presence of an explanation with how much detail the first text supplied. | In Experiment 1, mean target-sentence reading time was 2,685.23 ms (SD 1,252.34) with an explanation and 2,904.04 ms (SD 1,388.90) without one. The estimate `b = .07, SE = .03, p = .016` is for log reading time without an explanation minus with one. In Experiment 2, explanations also shortened reading time. The model comparison for integration of the two texts in free recall was `χ²(3) = 4.45, p = .22`. [Experiments 1–2, Tables 2–4](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11145-016-9630-3.pdf#page=6) | **Design.** The paper is licensed CC BY 4.0 and Table 1 includes translated English examples. The 30 complete Dutch stimulus sets and trial-level timing and recall data were not located. The two experiments draw on the same materials, and they can inform separate checks of local processing and delayed recall. [Journal article and license](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11145-016-9630-3) |
| **R11** Chi et al. (1994) | 24 English-speaking grade 8 students read the same expository text about human circulation. The researchers prompted 14 to explain each line to themselves and had 10 read twice to roughly match task time. Participants completed pretests and posttests on circulation. Complex questions required inferences across components or applications of circulation principles to health problems. | On complex questions (Categories 3–4, with up to 6 points per question and partial credit), the proportion of points earned increased by 22.6 percentage points in the prompted group and 12.5 percentage points in the unprompted group. The difference in gains was 10.1 percentage points, `t(22) = 2.64, p < .01`. [Method and Results, Fig. 3, p.453](https://education.asu.edu/sites/g/files/litvpz656/files/lcl/chideleeuwchiulavancher_2.pdf) | **Design.** Line positions align with prompts and codes for cross-sentence inferences. The comparison concerns an instructional interaction. The paper provides sample questions, verbal records, and mental-model summaries. Complete records, textbook stimuli, and reuse permission remain unconfirmed. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3) |
| **R12** McNamara (2004) | 42 college students were randomly assigned in equal numbers to self-explanation training or reading aloud. After reading English training texts, both groups read the same low-cohesion mitosis text aloud and explained it sentence by sentence. The preceding text stayed visible when it fit on screen. Post-reading open-ended questions were scored separately for the proportion correct on text-based and bridging-inference questions. | The question-type × training × knowledge interaction was `F(1,38) = 4.17`. For readers with low prior knowledge, the training advantage on text-based questions was `F(1,19) = 11.07`, with `Cohen’s d = 1.92` reported in Fig. 1. For bridging questions, the main effect of knowledge was `F(1,38) = 20.79`. [Posttraining Session: Comprehension, Fig. 1, pp.14–15](https://andymatuschak.org/files/papers/McNamara%20-%202004%20-%20SERT.pdf#page=14) | **Design.** Evaluate question types separately within the prompting conditions. The paper supplies sample questions, coding rules, and a summary of verbal analyses for 26 participants. Full self-explanations, stimuli, and reuse permission remain unverified. The low-cohesion posttest text came from McNamara (2001), so including it requires checking the two sources together. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326950dp3801_1) |
| **R13** Recht & Leslie (1988) | 64 students in grades 7–8 were grouped by baseball knowledge and reading ability measured before reading, with 16 per group. Participants silently read the same English account of a half-inning in five segments. After each segment they reenacted play on a model field with figures and retold what happened. At the end they wrote a summary. | The maximum score for correctly reenacted actions was 40. Among higher-ability readers, the high- and low-baseball-knowledge groups averaged 31.4 and 18.8 (SDs 3.9 and 7.4). Among lower-ability readers, the corresponding means were 27.5 and 13.9 (SDs 5.1 and 5.7). The main effect of knowledge was `F(1,60) = 85.00, p < .001`. The overall multivariate knowledge × reading ability interaction was `F(8,53) = 0.43, p > .05`. [Method, Reenactment Measures, Table 1, pp.17–18](https://www.yesataretelearningtrust.net/Portals/0/Effect-of-Prior-Knowledge-on-Good-and-Poor-Readers-Memory-of-Text.pdf#page=2) | **Design.** The same text allows comparison across levels of domain knowledge. Grouping by existing knowledge provides associational evidence. Published summaries and scoring rules were checked. Full materials, trial-level data, and reuse permission remain unconfirmed. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.80.1.16) |
| **R15** McKeown et al. (1992) | English-speaking grade 5 students read four passages about the American Revolution. The research report describes 48 participants who all received the same background knowledge unit, then were assigned the original or a more coherent revision. The researchers also compared the results with data from an earlier revision study. Outcomes were recall and comprehension questions after each passage. | For the group of 48, mean recall of content units common to both versions was 16.4% on the original and 24.8% on the revision, `F(1,46) = 7.59, p < .01`. Comprehension scores were 38.5% and 53.5%, `F(1,46) = 6.08, p < .05`. Table 2 shows that effects on both outcomes varied across the four passages. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.2307/747784) · [Archived author report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED410308.pdf) |
| **R43** Yeung et al. (1998) | Experiments 4 and 5 used the same English passage and compared printing word meanings above target words with providing a glossary at the end. The participants were grade 8 English learners in Hong Kong, with 56 in the lower-ability sample and 57 in the higher-ability sample. Each experiment split its sample across the two layouts. Readers could consult the unannotated passage during the comprehension test. The study also measured recall of word meanings and subjective difficulty on separate 1–9 scales for the text and vocabulary. | The proportion of comprehension questions answered correctly with inline meanings and the glossary was 23.57% and 21.79% for lower-ability readers, and 22.07% and 30.00% for higher-ability readers. Recall of word meanings in the same order was 36.31% and 63.39%, and 37.93% and 31.25%, respectively. The layout × question-type interactions in the two experiments were `F(1,54) = 29.89` and `F(1,55) = 8.01`. For subjective difficulty, the same interaction was `F(1,54) = 1.18` (not significant) and `F(1,55) = 4.09` (reported as significant), respectively. [Experiments 4–5, Tables 5–6, pp.12–17](https://www.academia.edu/99753423/Cognitive_Load_and_Learner_Expertise_Split_Attention_and_Redundancy_Effects_in_Reading_with_Explanatory_Notes) | **Design and aggregate comparison.** The paper includes the single stimulus and example questions from Experiment 1. Experiments 4 and 5 shared stimuli. The complete versions, trial-level data, and reuse permission remain unverified. The interactions compare text and vocabulary outcomes within each experiment. The study did not test an interaction directly across the two ability groups. [Journal article](https://doi.org/10.1006/ceps.1997.0951) |

## Contrasts to retain

- **R04 Causal explanations and open-book inference.** Compare the original text and the version with causal explanations using the same questions and scoring rules. In Experiment 1, each answer received 0–3 points based on completeness, and the total for the seven questions rose by **4.0 points out of 21**. Participants took the test one week after reading and could consult the text. Record the manipulated region, the textual evidence needed for the answer, and the inference the reader must supply. Conceptual overlap also improved open-book inference in Experiment 2, so other rewrites should not automatically be assigned a zero effect. [§§2.1.2–2.2, 3.2, Appendix](https://www.academia.edu/57360367/Some_good_texts_are_always_better_text_revision_to_foster_inferences_of_readers_with_high_and_low_prior_background_knowledge)
- **R01 Prior knowledge and problem solving.** Compare the proportion of points earned on the problem-solving comprehension questions across the highest- and lowest-coherence versions. The low-knowledge group went from .17 to .30, and the high-knowledge group from .61 to .46. The difference between those changes is **28 percentage points**, calculated from the four cells in Table 2. Retain the two rounds of post-reading measures and the knowledge strata. The highest- and lowest-coherence versions were 1,053 and 683 words. The coherence manipulation also changed length and the amount of explanation. [Experiment 2, Methods, Table 2, Fig. 4](https://www.academia.edu/783348/Are_Good_Texts_Always_Better_Interactions_of_Text_Coherence_Background_Knowledge_and_Levels_of_Understanding_In_Learning_From_Text)
- **R10 Earlier explanations and delayed recall.** With the inconsistent sentence fixed in the second text, the group that read an explanation in the first text read the target sentence **218.81 ms** faster on average. Experiment 2 used the same stimulus family and again found a target-sentence reading-time difference, with no corresponding evidence of improvement in cross-text integration during free recall. This study can inform separate checks of a localized processing signal and post-reading integration. To measure whether readers understand why the inconsistency can be resolved, ask them to state the connection. [Experiments 1–2, Tables 1–4](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11145-016-9630-3.pdf#page=6)
