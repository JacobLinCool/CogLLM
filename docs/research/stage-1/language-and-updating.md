# Stage 1 · Linguistic Cues, Text Integration, and Comprehension Updating

| R29 | Xue et al. (2026). 42 native Mandarin speakers read sentences in simplified Chinese. The study excluded 1 participant for scoring below 70% on comprehension checks. It manipulated the visual complexity of two-character target words and whether they occurred mid-sentence or sentence-finally. For fixated target words at position 0, the model used 2,015 observations from 79 words and 41 readers. | At position 0, the difference between sentence-medial and sentence-final positions was +3.99 ms for first-pass gaze duration (95% CI [−15.70, 23.69]) and +86.23 ms for total fixation time ([52.93, 119.54]). The two eye-tracking measures differed. [Results, Fig. 1, Table 5](https://borogjoon.github.io/Yaqian-Bao/Papers/xue-2025-no-wrap-up-effect-chinese-reading.pdf) | OSF provides an eye-tracking export and R analysis scripts. The data documentation lists the complete stimulus sentences, base sentence IDs across versions, the comprehension question text, and conditions. The project lists no license. The target word and language can be retained for an auxiliary comparison of reading at sentence ends. Sentence comprehension requires corresponding comprehension questions. | R30 | Smith & Levy (2013). In the English Dundee eye-tracking corpus, 10 readers each read 51,502 words of British newspaper text. The American English Brown word-by-word self-paced reading corpus had 35 readers, with 3 excluded. The researchers estimated conditional word probabilities with a trigram language model, then fitted reading times at the current and following words, controlling for factors including word frequency. | The first-pass eye-tracking effect appeared on the current and following word. In button-press self-paced reading, it appeared on following words 1–3. Summing the effects over those positions within each method yielded an approximately logarithmic relation over at least six orders of magnitude in probability. The bootstrap 95% CIs for the quadratic log-probability term were [−.45, .37] for eye-tracking and [−.90, 1.07] for self-paced reading. [§§3.1–3.3 and 4, Figs. 3–4](https://www.mit.edu/~rplevy/papers/smith-levy-2013-cognition.pdf#page=6) | The paper identifies both corpora. Trial-level files and permission to reuse the newspaper and Brown texts remain to be checked. Surprisal and measurement lag can serve as auxiliary controls alongside sentence comprehension and question reports. |
| R31 | Christianson et al. (2001), Experiment 2. 24 native English-speaking undergraduates each read 40 target sentences and 92 fillers. Each reader saw one version per target sentence. The researchers crossed clause order and the position of the head of the ambiguous noun phrase. After the whole sentence, a question asked about one of the roles. | Mean error was 55.6% for subordinate-clause role questions and 9.4% for main-clause role questions, with by-participant F(1,23)=70.08 and by-item F(1,39)=165.73, both p<.001. The correct responses for the two question types were “no” and “yes,” respectively. Within subordinate-clause questions, where the correct response stayed fixed, error rates across the four conditions still ranged from 47.5% to 73.3% (Table 5). [Experiment 2, Methods, Results, Table 5, pp.382–385](https://ferreiralab.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/222/2015/05/Christianson-et-al.-2001_LingeringInterpretationsGP_Cog-Psych.pdf#page=15) | Appendix 1 gives the base stimuli. Full trial-level responses and separate permission to reuse them remain unverified. A balanced set of rewritten questions could test whether readers revised a specific thematic role. The original question design can also help distinguish the disambiguation point from post-reading comprehension. |
| R32 | Camblin, Gordon & Swaab (2007), Experiments 1–3. English passages crossed whether a target word was related to a nearby associate with whether it was congruent with the more distant discourse. Experiment 1 aligned ERPs to target-word onset. Experiments 2–3 recorded eye movements in the target and subsequent regions. Experiment 3 placed targets mid-sentence to avoid a sentence-final confound. | For the midline target-word N400 at 300–500 ms in Experiment 1, the analysis of 24 participants reported F(1,23)=21.37 for discourse congruence and F(1,23)=15.49 for lexical association (both p=.001 in Table 4). In Experiment 2, the discourse effect included regression-path duration in the target region. In Experiment 3, the lexical association effect varied with discourse congruence. [Experiments 1–3, Table 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1766924/) | The paper gives example texts and the manipulations. Full trial-level files, stimuli, and reuse permission remain unverified. Design independent contrasts for distant context and local association, for example by fixing the words in the sentence while changing the situation that the preceding text establishes. |
| R33 | Claus & Kelter (2006), Experiment 3. 40 native German-speaking undergraduates read 24 target narratives. Events appeared in the order E2, E3, flashback E1, and E4. The study varied how long E2 lasted in the story. Readers recognized the probe word from E1 after the narrative returned to its main timeline, and its position in the text stayed fixed. | Mean correct recognition latencies were 2,027 ms for the longer event and 1,960 ms for the shorter event, a difference of +67 ms (the original 95% CI for the difference had a half-width of 67 ms). The by-participant p=.049 and by-item p=.09. The sentence returning to the main timeline, which preceded the probe, was read 68 ms faster, in the opposite direction from the recognition-time result. [Experiment 3, Methods, Results, Table 6](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6850733_Comprehending_narratives_containing_flashbacks_Evidence_for_temporally_organized_representations) | Table 5 gives a translated example. Full German texts, trial-level files, and reuse permission remain unverified. Sentence-level tasks can vary the order of events and test event-cue retrieval. Keep both distance in the text and distance in story time, and verify the effect on new materials. [Publication record](https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.32.5.1031) |
| R34 | Cozijn, Noordman & Vonk (2011). Two Dutch-language experiments shared 24 short texts. The target was a pair of causally related sentences with or without a causal connective. Each analysis included 40 readers. Experiment 1 measured region reading times. Experiment 2 also asked readers immediately to verify a statement that required an inference from the sentence pair. | In Experiment 2, with and without the connective, reading times were 377 and 414 ms in the first target region and 581 and 547 ms in the sentence-final region. Mean correct inference-verification times were 2,317 and 2,406 ms. The verification-time analysis excluded the 7% of trials with incorrect answers. The verification-time contrast was F(1,38)=11.42 by readers and F(1,22)=15.81 by items. Table 4 also reports intervals and a combined test. [Experiments 1–2, Methods, Tables 3–4, pp.482–493](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/files/1442185/cozijn_noordman_vonk2011.pdf) | The paper includes example materials. Full stimuli, trial-level files, and reuse permission remain unverified. Reconstruct a test with and without a marker for the same semantic relation, and compare region reading times and inference-verification speed separately. |
| R35 | Kleijn, Pander Maat & Sanders (2019). 794 Dutch-speaking adolescents each completed four HyTeC cloze tests drawn from versions of 20 authentic texts with more or fewer connectives. The authors analyzed cloze performance across entire texts and within sentences containing a manipulated connective. The local analysis included 183 items and 38,214 responses. | In the local model, the fewer-minus-more-connectives effect varied by relation on a log-odds scale. The effects were −.207 for contrastive, −.078 for temporal, and +.105 for additive relations. Causal relations were the reference category at −.060 (SE .031, p=.053). Relative to causal relations, moderation was significant for contrastive and additive relations and nonsignificant for temporal relations. The full-text model detected no overall connective effect. The effect in difficult texts came from a post hoc analysis. [Results, Tables 5 and 7, pp.455–458](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/0163853X.2019.1605257) | The appendix describes the manipulated relations and materials. The article is marked CC BY-NC 4.0. Reuse permission for the full paired texts and trial-level responses remains unverified. Use the study to design sentence-level comparisons stratified by the presence of a connective and the relation type. Report full-text scores separately. |
| R37 | Wu & Mo (2010), Experiment 3. 60 native Mandarin-speaking undergraduates read expository passages of about 230 Chinese characters, with 16 target passages and 12 fillers. The study varied whether two sentences intervened after a causal premise and whether the conclusion was consistent with that premise. Each reader saw one version of each passage. The study recorded reading times at the conclusion sentence and at the next sentence. | At the conclusion, the mean reading-time difference between inconsistent and consistent conditions was +343 ms for adjacent sentences and +159 ms with two intervening sentences. The main effect of inconsistency was F(1,59)=18.918 by readers and F(1,15)=6.968 by items, with p=.019 for the latter. The distance × inconsistency interaction had p=.037 by readers and p=.281 by items. At the next sentence, neither analysis detected a significant interaction. [Experiment 3, §§4.2–4.3, Tables 10 and 12, pp.209–211](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlxb/EN/article/downloadArticleFile.do?attachType=PDF&id=210) | Table 10 and the surrounding material provide examples. Trial-level files and permission to reuse the full materials remain unverified. Use the conclusion to test for behavioral spillover from cross-sentence inference. Verify the effect of distance independently before training on it. The post-reading probe measures word recognition. |
| R38 | Scholman, Yan, Yung & Demberg (2026). The Mandarin region-by-region self-paced reading experiments retained 78 and 116 participants in Experiments 1 and 2, respectively. The studies used 24 three-sentence passages and varied the presence of a first connective and whether the relation was causal or concessive. Experiment 2 removed an intervening additive clause. | The first connective did not consistently facilitate reading at the second connective. The interaction at the second connective in Experiment 1 was β=.04 (95% CrI [0.00, .08]), in the direction opposite to the prespecified expectation. Experiment 2 did not reproduce it. The exploratory pooled estimate was β=−.02 ([−.05, .02]). β is the interaction coefficient in a lognormal reading-time model. Subsequent regions are reported separately. [Experiments 1–2, Figs. 1–5, Tables 7–8](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-026-10269-2) | The OSF project lists CC BY 4.0. Each of the two materials tables contains 24×4 versions and separates the connective from the two subsequent regions. The released analysis file for Experiment 1 has 5,428 region observations with reader and stimulus IDs, order, reading time, and condition. This supports auxiliary evaluation of model estimates against observed reading times by region. The study used comprehension questions to exclude readers. Model estimates of reader questions require validation against reader reports. | R39 | Yu, Hsiao & Lin (2025 online, 2026 issue). Native Mandarin speakers read short dialogues and made a two-alternative forced choice. The tested scales included some/all, or/and, warm/hot, and good/excellent, in three contexts. | R40 | Wei, Tang & Privitera (2024). 29 and 34 native Mandarin-speaking undergraduates completed region-by-region self-paced reading and full-sentence eye tracking, respectively. The samples did not overlap. The researchers crossed grammatical and semantic acceptability to create four versions of each of 80 bǎ-construction sets and assigned them by Latin square. Each reader saw 80 targets and 100 fillers. | In self-paced reading, syntactic violation effects appeared at the critical word and the next region (p=.015 and p<.001), and semantic violation effects appeared two regions downstream (p=.041). That method did not detect a syntax × semantics interaction. Eye tracking detected an interaction in total reading time at the critical word, t(303.48)=−4.29, p<.001, and in total sentence reading time, t(321.32)=−2.34, p=.020. [§§2.1–3.2, Fig. 1, Tables 3–4](https://doi.org/10.1017/langcog.2023.42) | The released CSVs were checked. The region-reading file has 13,920 rows and the eye-tracking file has 13,600. Both have reader and stimulus IDs, regions, conditions, and times. These are released row counts. The observations used to model an endpoint depend on its exclusion rules. The OSF project lists no license. The same stimuli allow comparison across presentation modes and visible regions. Eye tracking captures processing before or at the critical word, while self-paced reading retains subsequent positions. The violation label describes the experimental manipulation. Subjective difficulty requires its own measure. | R42 | Daneman & Carpenter (1980). In the English reading-span task, participants read sentences aloud and recalled their final words in order. The 20 college students in Experiment 1 also read 12 narratives silently. After each narrative, they answered factual questions and questions about the referents of sentence-final pronouns. | In Experiment 1, reading span ranged from 2 to 5 sentence-final words. Reading span correlated with accuracy on factual questions at r=.72 and on reference questions at r=.90, with both p<.01. The endpoints pair a reader-level measure with post-reading question performance. [Experiment 1, Tables 1–2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(80)90312-6) | The paper demonstrates an independent measure of readers' working memory capacity. A full reusable question bank and trial-level files remain unverified. The variable stays unknown when the reader background is empty. |
| R44 | Love, McKoon & Gerrig (2010). The study used 24 sets of English narratives. Experiments 1 and 2 included 34 and 20 college students, respectively, who recognized names or other target words at specified points around a character's introduction. Experiments 3 and 4 each included 20 readers who completed recognition tests after reading the whole story. The tested words appeared before or after the character introduction. | In Experiment 1, mean recognition time for the character name was 925 ms without a stated relation to the story and 958 ms with an explicit relation. In Experiment 4, for delayed recognition of words following the introduction, the corresponding means were 862 and 790 ms (+72 ms), F(1,19)=10.2 by readers and F(1,23)=7.5 by items, both p<.05. Corresponding accuracy was .64 and .66, and the main result concerns speed on correct trials. [Experiments 1–4, Tables 4 and 7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2864939/) | The paper gives identifiable stimulus examples and positions. Full stimuli, trial-level files, and reuse permission remain unverified. Recognition tests at two times can distinguish the accessibility of the unresolved character from memory for subsequent information. Moving the clue earlier or later is a controllable manipulation. The onset of reader confusion needs an independent measurement. |
| R46 | Kendeou, Smith & O’Brien (2013), Experiments 6–7. English passages provided an earlier claim and a later update. In Experiment 6, 40 college students read conditions that included three sentences of causal explanation or three sentences of qualifying elaboration. The study measured reading time at targets following the update. In Experiment 7, 40 college students read 16 target passages and verified the same earlier information after the background or after the target sentence. | In Experiment 6, first target-sentence mean reading times were 2,159 ms with the original inconsistency, 1,893 ms with qualifying elaboration, and 1,873 ms with causal explanation. In Experiment 7, from after the background to after the target, verification time for the earlier claim fell from 2,356 to 2,083 ms with qualifying elaboration and from 2,313 to 2,303 ms with causal explanation. The interaction was F(1,36)=8.01 by readers and p=.09 by items. [Experiments 6–7, Tables 5–6, pp.859–861](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230587300_Updating_During_Reading_Comprehension_Why_Causality_Matters) | The author-posted full text includes stimulus examples. Trial-level files, full stimuli, and reuse permission remain unverified. Use this design to test whether reading delays and reactivation of an earlier claim separate in time after a correction. Verification time reflects accessibility of earlier information. Whether readers believe that information requires a separate measure. [Publication record](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029468) |
