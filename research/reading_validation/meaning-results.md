# Does a sentence supply a learned word’s meaning?

In the third Dutch vocabulary experiment, 41 participants judged whether previously studied Swahili words fit their Dutch sentences and expressed their confidence. We recovered 16,277 of 16,400 responses across 397 of 400 presentations and 100 words. Participants endorsed fitting words in 87.2% of familiar contexts and 78.5% of unfamiliar ones. Endorsement of nonfitting words was 10.6% and 10.5%, respectively. The six-level response was reconstructed from the correctness-oriented source coding and checked against both reported accuracy and confidence on every trial. Each held-out score used a reader and word excluded from fitting, and any matching target-masked test carrier was removed from training. [Primary study](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12285) · [Protocol](meaning-protocol.md) · [Original archive and sources](exposure-results.md)

Before seeing the Swahili word, the frozen model favored the true Dutch gloss onset more strongly in the fitting than the nonfitting context for 89.9% of 99 familiar-context word pairs (95% paired word bootstrap 83.8–94.9%) and 83.2% of 95 unfamiliar-context word pairs (75.8–90.5%). This prior estimator transferred from earlier experimental materials to a separate cohort and task. The raw whole-sentence state predicted human responses less accurately than a surface model (binary log loss 0.767 vs 0.696). Prefixing the Dutch gloss improved the score to 0.728; its advantage over the raw sentence did not survive uncertainty measured across words. Substituting the correct Dutch word into the source produced 0.639 but changed the input the participant saw. Adding the glossary-state features to an offline model that knew experimental fit and practice also failed to improve it (0.422 vs 0.403). A follow-up used the known word’s original token location and gloss. The target-specific scalar was read out before the foreign word during a single pass over the unchanged sentence. Its held-out log loss was 0.601 compared with 0.696 for the form model. The average improvement was 0.095 across words (95% interval 0.073–0.117) and across readers (0.084–0.105). Its item-level rank correlation with endorsement was 0.507, falling to 0.188 within fitting and −0.027 within nonfitting items. This performance largely distinguished fitting from nonfitting contexts. It required a supplied experimental term–gloss pair, and we chose the localized readout after inspecting the primary third-cohort results. The study has no token-resolved timestamps of individual meaning resolution.
