# Can informative text change what a reader can infer at the next unfamiliar word?

The Dutch word-learning experiments by van den Broek and colleagues randomly distributed previously studied Swahili nouns between practice conditions, balancing prepractice knowledge in Experiment 2. A rich condition placed a diagnostic Dutch sentence before a shared carrier sentence containing the Swahili noun. In the retrieval condition the shared carrier appeared alone. In a third condition the same rich sentence appeared after a translation had already been submitted. The archive supplies the two authentic sentence orders, the noun and translation, item-level practice accuracy, and learners' individual prepractice knowledge ratings. The published positive average effect is already known. Register the following item-level tests before individual response values are read or fitted. [Primary experiment](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12285) · [Open data, codebooks and materials](https://osf.io/eujyn/)

## Prior predictions

Use the original Experiment 2 passages, N=102 nouns, and the pinned Qwen3.5-2B causal language model. These are Dutch experimental sentences with a Swahili target, an out-of-language transfer beyond our English textbook evaluation. The first independent measure is the strength of the Dutch meaning a reader could anticipate immediately before seeing the unfamiliar form. On this stop, the target's Swahili form and any following text have yet to enter the receptive field. For each original word prefix, read the native next-token distribution over the distinct first subword onsets of the complete prespecified Dutch translation vocabulary, with normal word-initial space. The held-out outcome grader knows the correct Dutch meaning. The model observes the whole original passage once under its causal mask and exports all 102 hypothesis trajectories together. Identical first subwords get the same score. Report an unambiguous-onset sensitivity check, winner accuracy, mean reciprocal rank (midranks), information in nats, and entropy change irrespective of which answer is correct. This finite-vocabulary measure depends on knowledge of the experiment's candidate inventory, and its limitation to word onset will be explicit.

Each true context and carrier share the same Dutch words immediately preceding the target. Prediction uses the native LM at the token immediately preceding the token containing the first Swahili character. Require that the naturally tokenized complete prefix ends on the preceding token's exact boundary. Compare rich-before against sparse and against the late-support condition at this common stop. The latter shares the entire visible prefix with sparse, so its prediction must agree to numerical tolerance. A higher target score under rich-before assesses whether the informative context supports this *specific* intended interpretation. Use effect size, positive fraction, and 10,000 word-cluster bootstrap intervals. As a harder semantic control, prepend another noun's disclosed rich sentence to the same shared carrier, matched by Qwen-token length without using responses. Derange within context length through minimum-cost perfect matching and allow no identical context or true target. These constructed foils have no human scores.

## Human endpoints

Use the first practice translation response provided by each participant in Experiment 2. Compute actual accuracy of the same noun and distribution of its recorded pretraining knowledge in each condition. Combine retrieval-only and retrieval-then-context for immediate accuracy because the diagnostic text arrived only after both responses. Report contrasts separately as a timing control. Primary behavioral association is the Spearman correlation across nouns between predicted rich-minus-sparse correct-meaning log score and observed rich-minus-sparse accuracy. Also report the correlation with the model's answer-independent entropy reduction. Bootstrap whole nouns for uncertainty, then cross both participants and nouns as a sensitivity analysis. Report the known main treatment effect separately, with its own denominator; a positive main effect alone is insufficient to validate item-by-item predictions. Report complete and unique-onset strata without selecting by accuracy. At the trial level, compare a simple prior-knowledge-and-condition estimate to the same predictors plus the model measure, using outer folds holding out participants and words simultaneously. Use a fixed ridge penalty of 1 for standardized predictors and an unpenalized intercept. Pair log losses in the held-out cells and bootstrap both clustering factors. Maintaining a position-invariant treatment effect or a constant difficulty per word cannot satisfy the differential item tests. Preserve human-session/paper coverage, package only public text, and avoid giving any reader ID, response or correct answer mapping to the accelerator.

For temporal generalization, link the two independently sampled cohorts through the Swahili spelling shared by Experiments 1 and 2. Retain only the matched rich condition and shared carrier whose exact text was provided by the Experiment 2 stimulus table. Experiment 1 includes inflection placeholders in the released uninformative scripts. Match the consistent concrete carrier extracted from the rich item, document coverage, and report independent-cohort endpoint predictions as a secondary replication; allow it when a material difference in sparse wording remains. Experiment 3 measures later sentence judgment and will not be treated as a second immediate reading response. A gain in practice translation does not identify the exact internal moment when an individual recognized the answer; there was only one response before feedback. This study supplies no signal for the size of a general-text cognitive-load curve.
