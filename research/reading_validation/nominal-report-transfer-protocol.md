# Can actual noun-reference choices predict whether a later sentence makes a reader stop?

An empirical noun pointer trained on original reader choices can recover nonidentical antecedents in new documents. A separate first-report model for experimentally changed premises has underestimated the actual effect of an earlier inconsistent noun. This follow-up tests a specific transfer hypothesis: weighting a semantic comparison by how likely game readers are to refer to that earlier noun should improve the model of spontaneous source-conflict reports. The original source click labels and all published experimental families were previously used for different tasks. Reader effects in the third cohort are known to the researchers. No third-cohort choice enters this estimator.

Capture an identical frozen word representation of each complete original experimental story and align its word endpoints. At each current noun, evaluate the probability of a backward reference versus a new noun using the game readout, and the probability of every earlier printed word conditional on a link. The source probabilities remain fixed. For every plausible earlier/current noun pair in a sentence, weight these two probabilities to obtain its reader-trained referential support. Estimate incompatibility from the separate original word-category judgments. Carry forward an observed reporting hazard for neutral and conflicting encounters and ordinary sentences. Compare the learned game weights with the original within-experiment noun-pair weights, replacing only the word-link evidence in the same model. Infer target timing from previous sentences and training stories only. Evaluate under the original family-omitted training and the third cohort of real readers. The primary study-specific endpoints are complete first-response path log likelihood and the unconditional difference at the identical target sentence. Source-only moment calibration and the probability-mass conservation check follow the original first-cue analysis. Disclose if recovering noun links in books and Wikipedia fails to improve actual reading responses in this intervention.
