# Predicting whether an argument contains enough support

The same language model gave different answers depending on which judgment it learned to predict. On 80 held Darmstadt essays, a readout trained on actual sufficiency votes lowered loss on 406 judgments from .457 nats for word surprisal to .381 nats with contextual representations. Across 2,445 held Webis logical-quality choices from four topics, the separate native readout scored .934 nats and surprisal scored .927. A gain for one source judgment did not transfer automatically to another. Three independent experts scored each of 302 previously held passages for local sufficiency, arrangement and clarity, providing 906 ratings per criterion. The table gives the proportion of comparisons in which the model correctly ordered different panel means within the same debate topic. The native readouts in the first three columns were trained before using any expert corpus ratings. The last two columns use exactly the same pairwise source as the third. The pairwise source readout reproduced several expert distinctions. For clarity its advantage over the form control was .062 [.027, .096], and over word surprisal it was .035 [.009, .061]. For local sufficiency the respective advantages were .024 [−.011, .059] and .016 [−.011, .043]. The three experts are a fixed panel; intervals resample the sixteen whole debate topics. We examined all three criteria. These are pointwise intervals, and comparisons with secondary source arms warrant independent confirmation. Two repeated source records were excluded under the corpus audit. Training on three of four expert-topic folds permitted native three-level probability forecasts on the fourth. For local sufficiency, the calibrated source-only readout scored .871 nats; a readout using the expert training topics scored .925, and adding the source to that readout gave .923. For arrangement the corresponding numbers were 1.009, 1.137 and 1.136; for clarity they were 1.034, 1.156 and 1.157. These passage-wide judgments support a comparison between whole arguments. They contain no observation locating the word on which a reader first needed more support.
