# What remains accessible after a new character appears?

Two original experiments let a reader follow the same opening into three different second paragraphs. The authors placed the same final sentence before their recognition probe. In the first experiment, 80 readers recognized an old protagonist's name after remention, a neutral continuation and a switch to a new character in 556, 654 and 757 ms. In the second, 88 other readers recognized an object originally associated with the protagonist in 943 ms after a remention and 984 ms after a switch. The 41-ms object result was weaker in the authors’ item analysis. The name and object banks share narrative material. They provide different probes and independent participant cohorts. [Original article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4266406/) · [Registered name comparison](character-access-protocol.md) · [Associated-object comparison](character-object-access-addendum.md)

| Probe and contrast in the expected human direction | Native target minus matched control words | Same contrast after subtracting inverse-distance access | Story families in the expected direction after controls |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Old name, neutral minus new protagonist | −.0215 | −.0154 [−.0516, .0223] | 12/32 |
| Old name, remention minus neutral | +.1395 | −1.2639 [−1.3724, −1.1578] | 0/32 |
| Associated object, old protagonist minus new | −.0001 | −.0001 [−.0248, .0229] | 14/32 |

Each estimate is the difference in log opportunity-adjusted retrieval at the same final reading region. The name remention adds an occurrence. The simple frequency-and-distance retrieval model therefore predicts a large remention boost, much larger than the one present in native attention. The exact associated object was never repeated in any manipulated paragraph. Both banks retained all 32 families; neither had a contiguous 20-word match to the earlier goal-change bank. Every narrative required one article pass. The largest unchanged-prefix discrepancy was 2.1 × 10⁻⁶. These original human effects show where a positive finding about changing *goals* fails to transfer to changing *characters* or their associated objects.
