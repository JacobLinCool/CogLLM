# Predicting how often two professionals disagree about the same summary

A later analysis addressed an error in the estimated rate of disagreement. In the initial held analysis, a summary's predicted probability of receiving a No response implied 7.13% disagreement between two independent readers, versus 2.85% observed between pairs of actual responses. We estimated a separate parameter for dependence between judgments of identical wording. This improved the likelihood of the observed held vote counts, although source uncertainty was substantial and the model still predicted more disagreement than readers exhibited. The test summaries had already appeared in the initial analysis. [Original summary study](transition-seahorse-results.md) · [Secondary analysis plan](transition-seahorse-repeated-protocol.md)

The source calibration used 449 published English ballots for 155 summaries from 146 training articles, requiring at least two definite responses per summary. Twelve summaries elicited both answers. Because a canonical source judgment may have been selected from these very ballots, we withheld all 693 canonical judgments from those articles when fitting the single-reader mean. This left 13,789 original judgments for fitting that mean. The original validation provided its calibration. Source repeat panels shared no article identifiers, exact summary strings or contiguous normalized 12-word runs with any canonical validation or test document. These precautions address the corpus authors’ warning about overlapping duplicate files. The official leaderboard comparison and released writer head use exclusively canonical training data. For a summary with estimated No probability *p*, the probability of two differing responses under our exchangeable beta-binomial model is 2(1−ρ)*p*(1−*p*). Under independence ρ equals zero. We fit ρ to the complete distribution of counts using the actual number of available ratings for each summary. The estimated dependence was .100. On the previously held 1,047 judgments of 351 summaries from 248 articles, 15 summaries elicited both answers. Resampling complete articles, the mean held count log loss fell from .1974 to .1896, a difference of −.0078 (conditional 95% interval −.0145 to −.0016). The pairwise Brier score also fell by .00094 (−.00166 to −.00027). Source dependence uncertainty changes the inference. Independently resampling both article samples gave a 95% ρ interval of 0–.390. Nearly one quarter of source resamples estimated zero dependence, and the corresponding held log-loss interval was −.0226 to .0000. The matched independent model trained after the article exclusions estimated 7.19% disagreement. Dependence calibration lowered that to 6.47%, still above 2.85% observed. Also fitting a new source intercept gave 6.37%, compared with 6.30% using the form observer and 5.53% under a constant-mean beta-binomial model. Native features improved the full count likelihood against that constant model (−.0567, −.1014 to −.0171), with an uncertain difference against the form observer (−.0078, −.0252 to .0096). Rater-language codes are available, but the supplement supplies no unique rater IDs for estimating person effects or determining participant overlap. A fitted ρ can capture case difficulty shared by judges, selection effects or residual variation within predicted risk. The records support a population-level analysis of repeated choices, with imperfect absolute calibration.
