# How likely are two readers to write exactly the same next word?

The frozen population observer assigns one probability to each of 8,192 named words and a residual probability to everything else. Its bound on exact-spelling disagreement can become uninformative. Condition each pair of distinct respondents on whether both supplied named words or both supplied unlisted words. Let r be the probability of an unlisted word, a the chance of identical spelling conditional on both words being named, and b the chance of identical spelling conditional on both being unlisted. The predictive probability of different spellings is 1 − (1−r)²a − r²b. Estimate r from the actual number of unlisted answers. Estimate a and b from original same-spelling pairs divided by all distinct-respondent pairs in the corresponding class. For the first conditional model use the original named collision probability and original residual mass, and for the second use the same two predictors. For r use the log odds of the original residual mass. Use ridge-penalized logistic regression, scaling each pair contribution by 1/(n−1) so one observed reader supplies at most one unit of effective weight. Each source prefix is scored by the frozen population observer that had not seen its sentence during base training. No external spelling or article may set the model parameters.

Fit a direct two-coefficient logistic calibration of full-spelling disagreement on the grouped forecast and an intercept-only source average as controls. Retain the original lower bound and its midpoint with the identification upper bound. Score all methods on the verified Provo adjacent-word updates and the 3,085 Peelle stems. The co-primary measures are absolute error in the magnitude of a signed adjacent-word change on Provo and absolute level error on Peelle. Compare the conditional split to the direct logistic calibration and the original lower bound, with simultaneous intervals within each study. Use paired whole-paragraph resampling in Provo and whole-stem resampling conditional on the recorded Peelle respondents. Preserve the released corrected spelling even when it falls outside the model vocabulary. Report exact positions, absolute direction accuracy and the change in mean level. These point estimates learn a population regularity. They do not narrow the mathematical identification bounds on an arbitrary division of unknown probability.
