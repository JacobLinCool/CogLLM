# How closely do independent groups of people agree on next-word disagreement?

On the same Peelle sentence stems, two disjoint groups of readers differed by **5.71 percentage points** on average in how often two responses had different spellings. The source-trained conditional model missed the same groups by **19.29 points**. Its excess absolute error over an independent human group was **13.58 points** [12.97, 14.21]. The model systematically predicted too much disagreement, with a mean signed error of +16.73 points [15.98, 17.46]. Reader sampling therefore gives a scale for interpreting the previously observed 19.15-point error against the full panel. [Registered precision analysis](spelling-human-precision-protocol.md) · [Numerical results](../results/reading-validation-spelling-precision/scores.json)

Researchers wrote short English sentences around selected target words to vary how predictable the ending would be. Readers saw the sentence up to the final blank and typed the first single word that came to mind. This task measures expectations at intentionally selected sentence endings. [Original stimulus construction and written instructions](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7406521/)

The chronological sample provided 297,927 distinct later responses from 233 anonymous people to 3,085 stems. We assigned 116 people to one group and 117 to the other, retaining the same person's assignment across every stem. We repeated this procedure 256 times and computed each group's disagreement from its own original normalized spellings. All 3,085 stems met the minimum of 20 responses per side in every partition. Stems had 65–101 respondents in total, with a median of 97. Individual halves contained 20–65 respondents, with a median of 48. For each target half we scored the other half and all three frozen source estimates against exactly the same responses. Each stem and each target half received equal weight. [Original sentence-completion norms](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-020-01351-1) · [Normalization and chronological linkage](reader_history_external.py)

| Estimate of a target half's spelling disagreement | Mean absolute error in percentage points [95% interval] | Mean signed error in points |
|---|---:|---:|
| Readers in the other half | 5.71 [5.61, 5.81] | 0.00 by symmetry |
| UCL conditional model for named and unlisted spellings | 19.29 [18.62, 19.97] | +16.73 |
| UCL direct logistic calibration | 18.96 [18.31, 19.62] | +16.06 |
| Constant disagreement estimated on UCL | 18.35 [17.71, 19.00] | +12.15 |

The source constant improved on the conditional model by 0.94 points [0.62, 1.25]. The conditional model's weaker external level calibration coexists with its separately measured gain on signed word-to-word changes in Provo. This Peelle comparison concerns levels at independent stems. The intervals resample 4,000 sets of whole stems while retaining the pairing of estimates. They condition on the 233 available people. Across the 256 random assignments, the middle 95% of the mean between-group absolute differences was 5.50–5.99 points. This measures the sampling agreement achievable by a second half of this panel. A model estimating the population from many observations could in principle be more accurate. Provo's public predictability table preserves response and total counts per word. It does not identify the same participant across prefixes, so the analogous whole-person split is unavailable there. Resampling the original assignment lists gave intervals of 5.59–5.83 points for the two reader halves, 18.47–20.12 points for the conditional model and 12.84–14.33 points for their paired difference. We recovered 61 distinct lists of 50 stems and one of 35 from the actual assignment records, with each of the 3,085 stems appearing in exactly one list. This sensitivity analysis was specified after the stem-level results were available. Some stimuli were constructed around the same intended ending. Neither interval estimates the effect of drawing a new reader population or an entirely new set of target words.
