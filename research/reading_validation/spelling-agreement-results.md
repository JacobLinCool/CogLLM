# Predicting when two readers write the same next word

Conditioning on whether two people chose named or unlisted words improved the estimated *size* of an update in readers’ expectations. A paragraph author could measure this quantity by asking different readers for the next word before and after a printed word and counting the fraction of pairs of readers who give different answers. The model instead computes both estimates in one causal pass through the printed paragraph. A decrease means readers became more likely to give the same continuation. It can happen when a difficult technical phrase becomes more predictable. [Source-only observation model](spelling-agreement-protocol.md) · [Original English norming study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13428-023-02261-8)

The eight coefficients describe three observed events. The first two predict whether a respondent writes an unlisted word. Three predict whether two named choices agree and three predict whether two unlisted choices agree. Each loss uses only real respondents at a prefix with the relevant response opportunity. The source fit used 135,905 choices across 1,726 word ends, each with its sentence excluded from the base readout's corresponding fit. With pairs weighted to avoid quadratic growth in influence with sample size, same-unlisted-word judgments had 1,661 effective opportunities. The largest fitted gradient was below 7 × 10⁻¹⁰. Every external forecast was sealed before opening outcomes for this estimator. Both external corpora had previously been examined for other estimators. The Provo result used 1,566 pairs of adjacent word positions in 46 paragraphs. On exact spellings, the conditional model reduced change error by 2.18 points [1.19, 3.17] relative to grouping unlisted answers, and by 1.01 points [.31, 1.72] relative to direct calibration. It recovered the direction of 73.9% of nonzero changes. Because the target is agreement over actual spellings, the data and metrics differ from those of the 8,193-category head. [Original Provo corpus](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-017-0908-4) · [Fit and score code](spelling_agreement_score.py)

On 3,085 independent Peelle stems from 233 readers, the conditional model reduced level error by 4.76 points [4.55, 4.98] relative to grouping. Direct calibration did better by another .34 points [.22, .46], and the source constant had the lowest error. The mean observed probability of two answers differing was 68.1%. The conditional model predicted 84.8%. The external difference is substantial even after correcting for vocabulary. The Peelle intervals resample complete stems conditional on the recorded readers. The two comparisons in each primary study have simultaneous 95% intervals within that study. These outcomes justify a word-level change forecast for similar English paragraphs. They do not calibrate individual effort or support an absolute difficulty scale across materials.
