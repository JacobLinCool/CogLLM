# Words that widen or narrow readers’ expectations

After reading a word, will people give a wider range of guesses about what comes next? On 1,566 consecutive positions in 46 independent Provo paragraphs, the Qwen3.5-2B readout tracked the signed change in disagreement between readers (Spearman ρ = 0.631, passage-bootstrap 95% interval 0.581–0.682). Seven empirical changes were exactly zero. Across the remaining 1,559, the readout predicted the direction correctly at 74.2% (71.7–76.8%). For the 601 changes of at least 0.20 in measured disagreement probability, it identified 260 of 296 rises and 269 of 305 falls. The reference is different people’s actual words at two consecutive prompts. It provides a measurable example of a curve moving up and down at the newly read word. Each participant supplied an actual next word. An observed probability of disagreement of 0.80 means that two responses sampled from different people at that prefix fell in different answer categories about four times in five. The model predicts 8,192 named spellings plus other, from a single causal encoder pass through the passage. For a prefix before word t, it reads the model state immediately before the incoming word. Subtracting this value from the value before word t+1 assigns the update to word t. The label says how much the population agrees about a continuation. A paragraph can be difficult despite an obvious next word, and a comprehensible sentence can permit many continuations. The contextual head gained 1.99 percentage points in update direction over calibrated Qwen (0.62–3.37). Its difference from raw Qwen was 0.64 points (−1.18–2.35). Its correlation advantage over calibrated Qwen was 0.012 (−0.001–0.025). Calibrating Qwen’s source response probabilities improved independent log loss by 0.212 nats per response (0.173–0.251) over the native lexical projection. The contextual head gained 0.201 (0.164–0.236) over raw Qwen and −0.011 (−0.022–0.0002) over calibrated Qwen. On 49,278 choices among the 7,275 unambiguous, whole, single-native-token words, the contextual gain was 0.060 (0.028–0.094) over raw and −0.021 (−0.033 to −0.009) over calibrated Qwen. This comparison removes frequency splitting between dictionary words that start with the same token. These outcomes separate a working behavioral measurement from an unestablished advantage of the more flexible head. The contextual head substantially improved log loss there, by 0.078 nats (0.069–0.087) over calibrated Qwen. All twelve individual fitted source projections met their stationarity criterion. The six full-population fits, including the final all-source fit used for transfer, had maximum normalized gradients below 0.00000029. The named and residual answer probabilities always sum to one. A forecast whose most probable category is other counts as a miss when evaluating prediction of an actual most frequent spelling. The mean observed exact-spelling disagreement was 0.745, compared with a contextual identified model range of 0.787–0.821. The readout tends to overstate population disagreement in this cohort. ## Independent serving check

The normal author-facing Gradio callback captured each of three examples in one encoder invocation. Changing the continuation left 21 shared earlier word forecasts within 0.00000033. A separate live L4 acquisition replayed 54 original Provo passages in 54 study forwards, with three additional numerical prefix controls. All 2,500 previously sealed modal categories matched. The largest discrepancy in disagreement probability was 0.000099, and the largest residual-mass discrepancy was 0.000074. The application can identify increases and decreases without generating an explanation or running an additional model. ## Identical words in different paragraphs

Across 11,452 pairs of identical printed words appearing in different original paragraphs, the contextual observer correctly ordered the two measured changes in population disagreement 63.5% of the time, averaging equally over 132 spellings (95% crossed paragraph-and-word interval 56.5–70.2). These comparisons involved 847 word occurrences in 46 paragraphs. A fixed word-identity score gives 50%; a print-form predictor trained outside each evaluated paragraph scored 47.4%. The contextual advantage over print form was 16.1 percentage points (6.2–26.3). Restricting both adjacent forms to lowercase ordinary interior words yielded 66.4% (58.8–73.2) across 8,952 pairs and 116 types. Raw Qwen scored 67.5% on all pairs, and the contextual observer's difference from it was −4.0 points (−10.5–2.5). This comparison shows variation with observed context at the very same printed word. It does not establish which earlier expression caused that variation or how hard readers found the passage. It uses the same previously examined Provo material and fixed forecasts. The open vocabulary also permits a distinct estimate of agreement on *exact* spellings. A source-fitted model learned the chance that two people choosing unlisted words had supplied the very same word. It reduced mean absolute error in the size of an adjacent update by 2.18 percentage points (1.19–3.17) relative to grouping all unlisted answers, and by 1.01 points (0.31–1.72) relative to direct calibration. Its live implementation was independently replayed on all 54 aligned original passages.
