# Readers pointed to unfamiliar characters. Can a model point to the same words?

In 39 held-out movie summaries, one pass over the original English text ranked the words readers identified as unexplained character references with average precision 0.800 [0.737, 0.859]. Only 1.87% of the eligible words were marked. At a development-set threshold, the model found 378 of 413 marked words and flagged 726 of 21,720 unmarked words. An author inspecting all alerts would find a reader-marked location in about one of every three alerts. Within the 212 segments known to contain a directly alignable character problem, the model's highest-ranked word lay inside a human-selected span in 201 (94.8% [91.9, 97.5]). This last result conditions on already knowing that the segment contains a problem. [Sealed forecasts and evaluation protocol](transition-snac-protocol.md) · [Held-out results](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/scores.json)

One held-out passage illustrates the mark. The narrative has followed Alfred and a group of bank hostages. A subsequent sentence names **Natascha** without having introduced her. Three votes mark that name unclear, and the model flagged exactly the eight characters at offsets 331–339 (end exclusive) in the released `tripod39` summary. The example was chosen after evaluation from correctly flagged words with at least two votes. It illustrates what one alert means. [Public summary and annotation archive](https://github.com/tagoyal/snac)

## Human observation and location

SNaC readers marked words or phrases that referred to characters they could not place in the narrative, or to events or objects they could not identify. The study presented segments containing roughly three sentences and recorded participant-selected spans. A marked phrase identifies what the reader found unclear after seeing the segment. Its start is not a time stamp for a private moment of confusion. The stimulus text was generated by summarization systems. All supervision in these experiments came from previously collected human judgments. [Goyal, Li & Durrett, EMNLP 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.emnlp-main.29/) · [Official data release](https://github.com/tagoyal/snac)

The model used 88 book summaries for fitting and 22 books for setting the alert threshold, grouped by source index across two summarizers. All 40 released movie summaries were held out from fitting. One 2,487-token summary exceeded the 2,048-token limit, leaving 39 independent movie documents for inference. The 149 eligible book/movie summaries supplied 89,231 words and 110,263 input tokens. The L4 spent 95 seconds extracting features with one backbone call per complete summary. The fitted character readout used 129 coefficients including its intercept. Its optimizer converged in nine iterations with a maximum absolute gradient of 4.7 × 10⁻¹¹. Each input word was checked against its standalone tokenizer prefix. Intervals below resample the 39 movie documents 10,000 times. [Extraction receipt](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/l4-forecast-receipt.json) · [Frozen supervised readouts](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/readouts.json)

| Prediction target in the movie test | Naming, repetition and position | Same controls + Qwen word surprisal | Local contextual word states | Local states + antecedent cues |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Average precision for an unclear character | 0.453 | 0.601 | 0.795 | **0.800** |

For characters the gain over lexical cues plus surprisal was +0.199 [0.136, 0.259]. When we added explicit native attention and similarity to possible preceding mentions to the local contextual state, AP rose only +0.005 [−0.003, 0.014]. The relation-specific criterion was unmet. The source had 302 eligible words covered by at least one span with two or more votes. Using this stricter target, contextual AP was 0.794. The data support a small model's ability to locate a particular kind of reader-identified problem. They leave its internal route to that result open. [Model contrasts](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/scores.json)

Event and object references were harder. Readers' spans covered 2,378 of 26,361 eligible words. The full readout's AP was 0.169 [0.126, 0.216], a +0.070 [0.043, 0.096] gain over lexical cues plus surprisal. It caught 686 of 2,378 marked words with 3,118 false alerts among 23,983 unmarked words. The 53.5% [47.2, 59.3] top-word result concerns 230 already-positive segments that contain both marked and unmarked words. Nine percent of eligible words were positive overall. [Word-level scores and source coordinates](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/RefE-aligned-outcomes.json)

## Is the detector simply finding new names?

After the primary result we added an English pretrained POS tagger and named-entity recognizer to the lexical and surprisal baseline. These taggers were given the complete reading segment, including words appearing after a scored location. The control also knew whether a full recognized person name had occurred earlier, the time since its last mention, the entity span length and the part of speech. Its probability readout used the same human training and calibration books. We specified feature types and subgroups before producing any movie tags. [Control design](transition-snac-entity-control.md) · [Original NLTK tools](https://www.nltk.org/api/nltk.chunk.named_entity.html)

| Evaluation words in the external movie summaries | Marked / eligible words | NER + POS + lexical + surprisal AP | Contextual model AP | Paired AP improvement, 95% interval |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| All eligible words | 413 / 22,133 | 0.663 | 0.800 | +0.137 [0.068, 0.210] |
| Within recognized person names | 198 / 764 | 0.812 | 0.926 | +0.114 [0.031, 0.206] |
| Within first appearances of recognized person names | 194 / 302 | 0.819 | 0.940 | +0.121 [0.037, 0.213] |

Human annotation and automated person recognition cover different populations of words. The NER tagger called 198 of the 413 positive word sites PERSON. Restricting the comparison holds predicted entity type, and in the last row novelty, constant. These post-primary diagnostics support information beyond automatic identification of a newly introduced name. They do not determine whether the readout retrieved the character's correct antecedent. [Per-subgroup results](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/entity-control-scores.json)

A stricter test compares two *occurrences of the identical name in the same movie*, one marked and one unmarked. With 125 such pairs of recognized person names from 27 films, the contextual model ranked 99.3% correctly and the NER control 98.9% (difference +0.4 percentage points [−2.2, 3.3]). Across 959 pairs involving already-repeated lexical forms of any type, from 15 films, the corresponding rates were 71.4% and 70.6%, with a wide difference interval. The conditional AP gains in the table above do not establish that CogLLM handles changes between repeated occurrences of the same name better than these controls. [Exact-form, within-movie comparisons](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/same-form-context.json)

## Repeated names and ambiguous annotations

The release records the selected string, which can occur more than once in a source segment. The primary character evaluation excluded 82 of 568 movie segments because at least one character mark there had multiple possible coordinates. It retained 486 segments and 22,133 of 26,417 words. A separate coverage analysis includes every one of the 554 released character span records. For a repeated string, an alert certainly intersects the reader's selected position if it covers every possible occurrence, and may intersect it if it covers any possible occurrence. The fixed model guaranteed an overlapping alert for 458 of 554 records (82.7% [78.2, 86.5]); up to 546 of 554 were compatible with an alert (98.6% [97.4, 99.6]). On the 123 ambiguous records alone those counts were 34 and 122. The sources cannot distinguish the exact outcomes in those cases. Of 431 unambiguous source span records, 424 overlapped at least one alert. A phrase can cover multiple words, and these counts differ from the 413 evaluable positive word sites. [Ambiguity bounds and fixed-threshold counts](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/span-ambiguity-bounds.json)

## Can the opening words forecast an abrupt scene change?

A separate model was trained on a binary response for the complete source-defined segment. On 529 noninitial movie segments, readers marked 266 as having an abrupt scene transition. From only the first five words, a raw relation readout ranked the segments with AP 0.624 [0.541, 0.710] and AUROC 0.609 [0.556, 0.665]. It caught 74 of 266 marked segments and flagged 34 of 263 unmarked ones. The contextual current-word model scored AP 0.673, versus 0.648 for surface and surprisal. The explicit relation model fell below the local-state model by 0.049 [0.005, 0.094]. After exposure to the complete incoming segment, the corresponding APs were 0.633, 0.638, and 0.602, respectively. Signals at the start contain some information about what a reader will later mark, with considerable error. The scene study provides a regional judgment and cannot date a token-level mental onset. ## Use in a reader-facing tool

The optional English narrative detector returns the original Unicode offsets and a frozen decision for each certifiable word. Its threshold of 0.04485 was set from book development data. On movie summaries 91.5% [87.1, 95.5] of confirmed positive word sites were detected, at 3.34% [2.99, 3.74] false alerts and 34.2% [30.8, 37.8] precision. It can leave a passage unmarked. The serialized head exactly reproduces all 26,417 archived word scores in the 39 movie summaries, including words outside labeled evaluation segments. It is optional for narrative writing because the released readers annotated summaries rather than general scientific prose. ## Reproduction

The source preparation downloads the authors' public 1.85 MB JSON and verifies its SHA-256. It uses the pinned Qwen tokenizer from the standard local Hugging Face snapshot. Run the source-only GPU module through the project's Colab worker. It writes `word-features.npz` and a hash receipt. The supervised readout, validation threshold, word-level scores, repeated-span bounds, pretrained name controls and optional app checkpoint can then be reproduced locally.

```bash
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_prepare
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_fit --features /path/to/snac-forecast
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_score
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_ambiguity
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_scene fit --features /path/to/snac-forecast
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_scene score
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_ner annotate
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_ner fit --features /path/to/snac-forecast
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_ner score
python -m research.reading_validation.transition_snac_export
```

The 39 movie summaries were kept outside all supervised fits. As with any study using a frozen language model and a previously public stimulus set, the backbone's full pretraining exposure is unknown. The reported intervals reflect sampling movies given the available participants and one deterministic fitted model. [Movie forecast seal](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/prediction-lock.json) · [Actual GPU extraction](transition_snac_forecast.py) · [Source and material membership](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/stimuli.json)

The separate L4 serving replay checked all 26,417 recorded words in the 39 movie summaries. It reproduced all 1,555 threshold decisions that flagged a word, with a maximum absolute score difference of 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁶. The 39 summaries and three technical inputs used exactly 42 encoder calls. A changed suffix moved prefix scores by at most 1.6 × 10⁻⁷; both composed and decomposed accented words retained their source spans. A punctuation-only input yielded zero alerts. [Verified serving replay](../results/reading-validation-transition-snac/l4-serving-receipt.json)

A separate source-book threshold search tested whether an author could request at least 70% trustworthy alerts. None of the 14 candidate thresholds supported that promise under the prespecified simultaneous, book-cluster lower bound. The highest qualifying-support lower bound was 61.3%. The default therefore remains a high-recall editing aid. On the known movies, 34.2% of flags matched an annotator’s mark [30.8%, 37.8%], and 91.5% of marked words were found [87.1%, 95.5%]. Training summaries came from two GPT-3 book summarizers and the external summaries from a BART-based movie-screenplay summarizer. Source genre and generating model changed together. ## Agreement about already-marked character words

Of 413 eligible marked movie words, 111 received one vote, 134 two and 168 three. A secondary, within-segment comparison held the annotator pool and sentence block fixed. Across 18 eligible segments from 18 movies (51 three-vote versus one-vote word pairs), the frozen Qwen score ranked the more widely marked word higher with probability 0.745 [0.565, 0.908]. The NER/surprisal control reached 0.657 [0.454, 0.843]; their difference was 0.088 [−0.106, 0.278]. Restricting further to first mentions of NER-identified people left six pairs from five movies; both scores ranked the more widely marked mention higher in half of comparisons. The full data release contains some four-vote span entries and gives no per-segment denominator. None of the eligible marked movie words in this analysis had a recorded count above three. This ordering concerns agreement about text locations after a segment was read. The available vote counts do not measure subjective effort intensity.
