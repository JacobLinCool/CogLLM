# Does the same pair of replacement words change order when its context changes?

Five proficient second-language English readers ranked potential replacements in each of 1,710 English Web contexts for the SemEval-2012 test. They could tie candidates. The organizers averaged annotator ranks, grouped ties and reported that readers sometimes changed their preference across contexts even when the choice of words stayed fixed. The source includes 171 targets presented in ten different contexts each. The published aggregate rankings support a material-level test of context sensitivity. They supply neither time-resolved load labels nor individual votes. We use only the test materials and original gold ranking from the task co-author's supplement. No SemEval training or model selection is allowed. [Original task and human rating instructions](https://aclanthology.org/S12-1046/) · [Co-author's data and historical comparison scores](https://github.com/sjauhar/simplex/tree/7242f641933707840b2884eedc0a53784942a440) · [Original reading texts, released by McCarthy and Navigli](https://aclanthology.org/S07-1009/)

**Eligibility.** Recover the 1,710 identified original Web sentences from the 2007 XML. Use the authenticated archive whose SHA-256 was independently recorded by the SWORDS authors. Reconstruct text from the head tag and exact surrounding text. HTML-decode the unambiguous XML character references only. Exclude malformed or uncertain source references, unknown or repeated marked-head boundaries, nonstandard test positions, and instances whose printed word differs by inflection from the supplied target lemma. The released candidate lists contain lemmas. Restrict the primary comparison to full single-word candidates compatible with our previously fitted source model; preserve source capitalization at the substitution. Never change the rest of the original sentence. Only candidate identities, never their prior suggestion counts or human ranks, enter the language-model input. The five readers saw the original context with a gap and a candidate list. Each filled text estimates a candidate under that question. Check original and rendered materials for exact or near duplicates and twelve consecutive words shared with historical CogLLM training/development, the Paetzold studies and the recent clinical/revision datasets. Exclude the affected context before any outcome is read. Retain counts and publish exclusion reasons. [Original 2007 archive checksum](https://github.com/p-lambda/swords/blob/main/swords/assets/semeval07.json)

**Source-trained estimators.** The previously frozen single-pass Paetzold readouts represent two distinct mechanisms. One learns a shared direction from direct unknown-word reports and three-way word-substitution choices; the other estimates these choices using lexical predictors and the conditional Qwen log probabilities of the replacement and up to three following words. Both were fitted before any SemEval outcomes were available. Quantify the contribution of each using a convex mixture of their three-way probabilities. Set its scalar coefficient exclusively by minimizing source-family-weighted ternary log loss on the earlier, already reported, 296 Paetzold revision validation families. Their role is now calibration for a new external study. Keep the mixture fixed when applying it to SemEval. For additional controls retain the previously trained lexical model, native target-only surprisal, raw English word frequency, the co-author's supplied word-frequency and adaptive contextual n-gram rankings, and the supplied frequency-backed psycholinguistic ranking. An individual word model or frequency ranking is independent of which other candidates happen to be in the list. No outcome-dependent term selection occurs.

**Primary.** Take two eligible contexts for the same source lemma and part of speech, and two identical, distinct candidate strings that appeared in both. Require a strict aggregate preference for candidate A in one context and B in the other. Give a model 1 if its signed preference for A increases in the context where A is human-preferred, 0 if it decreases, and ½ if unchanged. A context-invariant frequency score yields exactly ½. Compare the mixture to this ½ and separately to the locked lexical-plus-native-surprisal model and the supplied contextual n-gram ranking. Average over context pairs for each word pair, over word pairs for each source lemma/part of speech, and finally equally over lemma families. Bootstrap whole lemma/part-of-speech families 10,000 times, seed 420113. Report strict double correctness separately, counting correctly identifying the preferred word in *both* contexts. Use a constant tie fractional treatment of ¼ for this secondary joint correctness. Also show the subset where the complete original candidate sets match, and report how many distinct contexts and lemmas support it. A result with fewer than 10 independent informative source lemmas is descriptive.

For assignment to ordinary unseen rewrites, include ½ for model ties when evaluating every strictly human-ordered within-context candidate pair. Average within context then source lemma. Distinguish this endpoint from a genuine preference reversal. The co-author's ordinal rankings are comparable across contexts only as an order; use their {-1,0,1} relative choices on both contexts. For the native models use the continuous difference between probabilities assigned to A and B. Also provide the same three-valued comparison for every system to control for confidence resolution. The original source gives no individual votes on this target, so the mean ternary log loss of individual readers is unidentifiable. Conclude evidence for a contextual signal only if the registered primary mixture-minus-½ lower 95% limit is positive; report superiority to the stronger source and n-gram controls separately. Archive all comparisons regardless of the direction.
