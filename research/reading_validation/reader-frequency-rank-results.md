# Does a particular reader favor a different next word?

Giving an English reader's word choices their own learned frequency preference changed the model's most likely named word on 1,312 of 212,395 later responses (0.618%). On these disagreements the person-specific model predicted 222 actual spellings correctly, and the equally informed population model predicted 160. Across all named choices, this increased accuracy by 0.029 percentage points (95% crossed reader-by-stem interval −0.008 to 0.068). On 4,730 German responses, the two models always ranked the same word first. An individual advantage in choosing the favorite word remains uncertain. The two languages differed at the population level. In English the frozen sharpness-only population model incurred a loss of 3.6778 nats per named choice. Adding a common frequency preference lowered the source-trained population prior's loss to 3.4507. Conditioning on the target person's history lowered it further to 3.4504. In German, 99.84% of the auditory source mixture weight fell on a zero frequency tilt. Held readings gave 4.4350 nats per named choice under both population models to four decimals. Vocabulary commonness supplies useful information for adjusting our English word probabilities to this task. Adding histories yielded much smaller changes. [Fitted source distributions](../results/reading-validation-reader-rank/frequency-source-model.json)

Every comparison uses identical context, printed-word inventory and base encoder states. The frequency scale comes from the language-specific wordfreq list, fixed independently of the held response data. Each possible choice competes with all 8,192 English or 4,096 German spellings. Five frequency preference modes adjust probability within each of seven word-probability sharpness modes. All 26 source mixture fits reached their registered stationarity threshold. A numerical check compared the candidate bound with exhaustive ranking across 2,460 artificial context–profile combinations. There were no mismatches, with up to four different winning words for a single input. The unfavorable German ranking result is compatible with the concentration of its empirically learned source prior. English targets came from assignments started after submitting the conditioning responses. The German target set comprised written-only items, disjoint from shared materials used for personal conditioning. Answers outside the fixed inventories do not adjudicate the identity of a named alternative. Both cohorts had already been assessed for the earlier response-mode experiment before this preference axis was registered. Their current estimates describe a follow-up on the limits of a previously observed effect.
