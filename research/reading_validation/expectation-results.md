# What readers expect at an unchanged point in a sentence

When four alternative nouns ended the same English context, a single-pass Qwen readout ranked the nouns by their human-rated plausibility in 81.8% of unequal pairs. The complete-word surprisal of the same backbone already achieved 81.3%. Word frequency, length, repetition, position and tokenization achieved 57.2%. The experiment held out 32 entire contexts, covering 128 endings and 191 unequal comparisons. The advantage over the word-form comparator was 24.5 percentage points [15.6, 33.8]; the difference from native word surprisal was 0.5 points [−2.1, 3.6]. Thus this endpoint supports a contextual expectation effect. The extra attention and semantic-competitor measurements did not establish a further gain over the native word probability. Each context receives equal weight. The plausibility scale ran from 1 to 5; the average absolute error on held ratings was 0.54 points [0.45, 0.65]. In the EEG study, all 50 participants contributed to the held subset. The 12,429 electrode records reduce to 1,381 participant–ending measurements before item averaging. For the separate reading-duration experiment, all 216 participants contributed to 11,664 held trials at 162 endings. A probability forecast at a noun can explain which interpretation readers accept while leaving substantial uncertainty about their physiological response or the time they spend. Five additional published N400 collections supply same-study and cross-study evaluations. Whole-item comparisons are unavailable for two designs with only one ending in a given material group; their absolute-error results are retained. Exact sentences, sufficiently long identical stems and their source families were excluded from the external EEG training pool if the target study used them. The original native run processed 2,518 distinct complete passages with 158 encoder batches on one NVIDIA L4. It included every word in 55 multi-sentence Frick passages for transfer. Article formatting and target coordinates were separately audited in each source. [Raw-source alignment and hashes](../results/reading-validation-expectation/input-seal.json) · [Estimator and forecast archive](../results/reading-validation-expectation/forecast/seal.json)

On twelve repeated Frick story families, a contextual reading readout trained on the other families predicted a 3.51-point increase in first inconsistency reports after an earlier premise was changed. The original semantic model predicted 4.54 points; the two reader cohorts showed 32.12 and 31.70 points. The single-word expectation finding therefore does not establish calibrated contextual monitoring over a whole story.
