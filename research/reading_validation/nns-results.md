# Locating words that English learners report they did not understand

On 200 held English sentences, a supervised CogLLM word score identified which of two words more readers marked as unknown in 86.43% of comparisons (95% interval 83.97–88.67%). A model trained on word frequency, word shape, curriculum level, position and previously observed stem difficulty reached 85.98%. The paired gain was 0.45 percentage points (−0.35 to 1.27). The prespecified criterion for added discrimination beyond this lexical control was unfulfilled. Measured reader proficiency carried clearer information about the absolute chance of an unknown-word report. Frequency alone correctly ordered 82.70% of unequal pairs. The frozen PLABA and CompLex single-word estimates reached 78.31% and 72.43%. At the same printed word across contexts, the text-only model's interval spans chance (44.33–63.53%). The data give considerable support for localizing which *word* tends to be unfamiliar in a sentence. They leave uncertain whether the model can distinguish when the context surrounding the same word changes comprehension. Participants contributing to different sentences may differ. [Estimator and prespecified controls](nns-estimator.md)

The authors asked learners to report every content word they personally could not understand, with an option to say all words were understood. The released counts yielded 40,754 aligned responses at 2,008 designated word positions. Readers flagged a word 1,861 times. For 652 positions the readers disagreed. A randomly drawn pair of readers disagreed on 6.48% of identical word–sentence instances under equal-material weighting. Always predicting that a word was known agreed with 95.45% of individual responses. Respondents' choices were therefore informative even among materials where almost everyone accepted most words. [Original archive](https://ghpaetzold.github.io/data/User_Studies_NNS.zip) · [Agreement and coverage](../results/reading-validation-nns/scores.json)

Only half of the model's top choices across **all** printed words had an observable, unambiguous target judgment. Among that half, 86% were marked by at least one respondent. Over all top choices, the fraction of readers who would mark the selected word is identifiable only within 14.32–64.32% because unjudged words could have any rate. This limitation matters for an author-facing highlight. The study records whether the reader said the word was unknown after reading. It supplies neither a timestamp for a breakdown nor evidence that a particular later phrase resolves it. [Prespecified bounds](nns-estimator.md)

The model used 109,015 sparse-role judgments from 80,363 uniquely aligned source word positions. Distinct sparse and dense materials were checked against the earlier supervised source inventory. One dense sentence matching prior revision work and one sparse sentence related to a dense item were excluded. Where repeated word forms made a response ambiguous, the word was dropped. All 200 held forecasts and all paired revision forecasts were sealed before either held response set was graded. One causal Qwen3.5-2B pass per sentence yielded word-end features, with three direct prefix checks and separate full numerical serving parity. The original archive lacks persistent reader or source-page identifiers. The results establish transfer to new sentence materials within these study samples. To reproduce the reported word-order accuracy, proficiency gain and gains from sharing the two human tasks, download the original ZIP. From the repository root, run `python -m research.reading_validation.nns_public_replay /path/to/User_Studies_NNS.zip` with Python 3.11+ and NumPy. In an empty working directory containing the original ZIP and the nine listed package files, the replay reconstructed the 40,754 word judgments and 7,335 substitution votes. Its four point estimates and eight 95% limits agreed to an absolute tolerance of 10⁻¹². Twenty-eight views of those sentences, including their six proficiency settings, reused the same native output.
