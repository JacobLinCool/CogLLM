# Predicting a reader's delayed meaning judgment

The third experiment of [van den Broek and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12285) measured whether a previously learned Swahili word fit a Dutch sentence. Participants encountered familiar and unfamiliar carrier contexts after retrieval or contextual practice. The source codebook describes a six-category response recoded for experimental fit, with 6 indicating a confident correct judgment. Test grades have not been inspected for this analysis. This cohort and judgment differ from our preceding two vocabulary-practice experiments. The original authors reported aggregate experimental effects, and our group has already studied the first two cohorts and their overlapping vocabulary. This is prospective within this project for the third cohort, with explicit prior knowledge of the earlier results.

## Inputs and interventions

Each original test sentence is presented as written. A second input prefixes the canonical Dutch gloss using the same Dutch declarative template for all 100 words. This prefix represents certain access to the meaning. Actual study participants had the opportunity to learn the word, with imperfect retention. A third, separate control replaces the Swahili word with its Dutch meaning in its original sentence. This is an idealized native-language comparator and has different tokenization. Each presentation uses exactly one causal forward with no question, decoding, or access to later trials. Before looking at grades, register the input hashes, word coordinates and model code. Exact target terms encountered only inside another string fail whole-word matching and are audited. Identical hosts after substitution of the target word by a common sentinel are removed from training when seen in the evaluation fold.

## Predeclared comparisons

1. Test an independent transfer of the prior study's token-onset estimator. Before the first native token of the Swahili noun, rank the first native token of its Dutch gloss among all unique Dutch target onsets. Compare the score at fitting and nonfitting test sentences for each word, separately for familiar and novel frames. Include all recoverable carrier contexts and report the proportion of exact input sentences with no preceding evidence. A fitting noun after an uninformative prefix can have an informative continuation, so this test only concerns pre-noun support.
2. After the sentence, predict the observed human fit judgment. Fit is the experimentally assigned direction. Reverse the source correctness-based six-point scale on no-fit items to recover a 1–6 scale where higher always means endorsing the word. Verify that the recovered sign equals experimental fit on precisely the correct rows and that reported confidence matches distance from the center. Raw keypress response reversal can differ by participant. Treat any undefined or unverifiable scale as missing and report it. The primary human endpoint is binary endorsement, scored by log loss. A secondary endpoint is the ranked probability score for the entire six-level distribution. Accuracy by condition, ordinal mean, and correlations within an experimental fit condition measure information beyond the average intervention direction. Reaction times do not identify the token at which a reader made a decision.
3. Compare a constant, form-only properties, native projected middle/final sentence states, and those states after a supplied glossary. A stronger oracle has design fit and familiarity and the known practice condition. Report the added accuracy of semantic states beyond this oracle. Ordinary form controls include sentence length, target length, relative target position and sentence diversity. Native article-only predictors use end states, article mean and surprisal without a marked target. The before-target onset score and lexical controls explicitly use the measurement locus. Secondary features conditioned on the location of the experimental term must be named separately.
4. For complete held-out word/person pairs, fit five by five blocked cross-validation. Hash words and participants separately with fixed salts and assign sorted hashes round-robin. A model cannot see the target word or the test reader's other responses during training. Exclude any sentences with a target-masked host shared with an evaluation item. Train only cumulative logistic models of the five binary thresholds, using a fixed mean-loss ridge penalty of .03 for standardized non-intercept coefficients and an unpenalized intercept. The middle threshold supplies binary endorsement. Monotonize the five cumulative probabilities per example by sorting. Train observations have equal mass per word then equal mass per observed response within that word. Include the two practice/familiarity factors (no gold fit) in the offline study-design comparisons, and label their availability. Do not represent unobserved people with learned embeddings.
5. Report cross-fitted individual NLL and RPS, with uncertainty using paired bootstrap means over words and separately readers. Participants and words are crossed, so require positive bounds on both for an individual endorsement claim. Distinct sources share wording across vocabularies. Report how many training rows and frames survive overlap removal. For source-only transfer report matched-word bootstrap intervals for the direction and item variability. Performance sign reversals, a win confined to recovering assigned fit, and substantial out-of-scope material remain in the report.

Model outcomes concern retrospective sentence-final semantic judgments in Dutch word learning. There is no human timestamp for when an individual meaning was accepted or rejected while reading these sentences.
